The Blaquier consignment, the Argentine family's collection that Sotheby's locked in this month for its November sale in New York, started showing specific numbers this week. The Van Gogh in the collection, a chestnut tree in blossom from eighteen ninety, comes estimated between one hundred and fifty and two hundred million dollars. A Cézanne harlequin could top one hundred and twenty million. To put that figure in perspective, Christie's recently sold another Van Gogh privately, a portrait of a Zouave from eighteen eighty eight, for around one hundred and ninety million, so the Blaquier numbers show the real level the market for major Impressionists and Post-Impressionists is trading at right now. The full collection remains valued at more than four hundred and fifty million between these two works and the Degas, Renoir, and Pissarro pieces alongside them. This is not the first time the family has surfaced in the New York market, last year Christie's had already offered two Signac paintings from the same collection in its November sale, so this fall completes a process that began nearly a year ago.

This week an old case also came back into circulation, and it is worth revisiting. In twenty twenty four, at the SP-Arte fair in São Paulo, an auction house founder publicly accused a painting attributed to Tarsila do Amaral, the Brazilian modernist painter, of being fake, a work being offered for around three million two hundred thousand dollars. The gallery selling it denied the claim, and months later the only expert authorized by the artist's estate ran X-rays and pigment analysis and declared it genuine, with a new valuation of sixty million reais, though a member of Tarsila's own family later came out publicly against that authentication. Tarsila holds the regional record for Brazilian painting, eleven million two hundred thousand dollars paid in December of twenty twenty for her work, so even a name at that level can end up at the center of a provenance dispute that science alone does not fully close.

Today's move is to let the two or three clients you have been keeping posted on the Blaquier consignment know that specific estimates are out now for the Van Gogh and the Cézanne, because those two figures give them a real yardstick for the scale of the November sale before the season kicks off. If any of them ask why this particular collection is getting so much attention, tell them those two works alone already add up to more than half of the collection's total valuation, so the rest of the catalogue is going to sell in the shadow of those two pieces.
