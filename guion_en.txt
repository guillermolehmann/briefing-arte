"Without a doubt, the most famous living artist in the world." That's how David Zwirner, the New York gallerist who represented Yayoi Kusama, the Japanese artist known for her polka dots and her infinity mirror rooms, described her on the day her death was confirmed, at a hospital in Tokyo, on August fourteenth, at age ninety seven. Good Sunday, Virginia, it's August thirtieth. Today effectively closes out the quietest month on the gallery calendar, because this coming Thursday, September fourth, the fall season unofficially kicks off in Chelsea, with Thursday night openings already starting to fill back up. Before that, Botero in New York continues at Sotheby's Breuer building location through September seventh, MoMA keeps its show pairing Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera open through the twelfth, and at the Whitney, painter Minnie Evans's retrospective, the museum's first dedicated to her since nineteen seventy five, runs through January. On September ninth, artist Danh Vo returns to White Cube with a preview, and on the twenty fourth the Armory Show opens at the Javits Center, this year focused on Caribbean artists and their diaspora, and handing out the fair's first two museum acquisition prizes in over thirty years of history, twenty thousand dollars for the Pérez Art Museum Miami and fifteen thousand for the Bronx Museum.

The underlying story this week is still Kusama's death, even though it happened more than two weeks ago, because only now is what she leaves behind starting to come into view. The Stedelijk Museum in Amsterdam, dedicated to modern and contemporary art, already had a retrospective of hers scheduled for September, and overnight it turned into a posthumous tribute, while Hong Kong, Tokyo, and New York saw tribute shows and memorial events pop up at the galleries that worked with her. What's still unknown is how the market responds, and that's exactly where your job comes in, because November's evening sales in New York will be the first real test of whether the death of such a highly valued artist pushes more owners to sell now, or instead shrinks supply because nobody wants to part with a piece just as its value might climb. Neither outcome is confirmed yet, so for now it stays an open question.

On another corner of the market, Christie's confirmed that this October, during Frieze week in London, it will auction a painting by Piet Mondrian, the Dutch painter of black grid lines and primary colors, unseen in public for more than twenty five years. It was painted in nineteen twenty and carries an estimate of twenty to thirty million pounds, nearly forty one million dollars at today's exchange rate, the artist's most important work to come to auction in Europe since two thousand nine. Before reaching London it will travel to Zurich, Paris, New York, and Hong Kong so prospective buyers can see it in person, proof that at the very top of the market, scarcity still moves more money than novelty. And at the Armory Show itself, this year's focus on Caribbean artists opens a side door for the Latin American circuit, with galleries like El Apartamento, from Havana, showing Cuban artists Alex Hernández Dueñas and Ariel Cabrera right in the section that will decide that first acquisition prize.

Today's move has two parts. If you have clients holding Kusama work, now is the moment to reach out and hear how they're thinking about the fall season, without pushing them to sell or to buy, just to be part of that conversation before they make up their own minds. And if you're tracking any collector with an interest in Caribbean or broader Latin American art, let them know now that the Armory Show opens on September twenty fourth with that focus, so they have time to look over the catalogue before the fair.
