New York's art market flips a switch this week. After an August that moved with the handbrake on, galleries are opening almost all at once, two hundred and eighty two shows between today and the weekend, the busiest window of the entire fall season. Good Tuesday, today is the eighth of September, and the flood we had been forecasting starts today.

The reason is calendar-driven. The Armory Show moved this year from its usual slot, the weekend right after Labor Day, to September twenty fourth through twenty seventh, to avoid colliding with Frieze Seoul. And since the holiday fell late this year, most galleries chose to open now, over these two weeks, instead of waiting until the last moment the way they have in other years. Tomorrow, Wednesday, from six to eight in the evening, the British gallery White Cube opens its preview of Danh Vo, the Vietnamese artist returning to show in the city for the first time since his Guggenheim retrospective back in twenty eighteen, with the exhibition open to the public the following day. On Thursday, the Swiss mega gallery Hauser and Wirth reopens both its Chelsea locations, closed for renovation, with the first New York show by the Brazilian artist Anna Maria Maiolino, a pioneer of Latin American conceptual art. On top of that, MoMA still has Frida and Diego, on Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera, running through the twelfth, and the self-taught painter Minnie Evans's retrospective stays open at the Whitney through January. By the time the Armory Show opens its doors on the twenty fourth, almost everything the city has to show will already be up on the wall.

On the market side, a small number that says something big. On July twenty eighth, the auction house Rago Wright sold the personal collection of the textile designer Jack Lenor Larsen, who died in twenty twenty, to benefit LongHouse Reserve, the sculpture and art garden he founded himself on Long Island around the idea that art, design and nature belong in the same conversation. In his lifetime, Larsen had bought a fiber sculpture by the Colombian artist Olga de Amaral, Tierra y Oro number five, wool, horsehair and gold leaf from the mid nineteen eighties, estimated between three hundred thousand and five hundred thousand dollars. It sold for nine hundred and sixty thousand, almost double its high estimate, and it was not the exception of the night, the sale as a whole placed ninety five percent of its lots and the total value closed sixty nine percent above what had been expected combined. It falls well short of the artist's record, three million one hundred twenty five thousand dollars paid last November at Christie's, but it confirms the interest did not stay stuck to that one record piece. There are buyers paying up for any Amaral work that comes up, even at a small benefit sale, and that is the difference between an isolated result and a market that has already pushed forward evenly, one more argument for any client sitting on a work by the artist who is unsure whether to consign it now.

Today's move is not to send the whole calendar, it is to build short lists. Pick two or three clients and write to each one with two or three specific names from this week, matched to what you already know they collect, Danh Vo if they lean conceptual, Maiolino if they follow Brazil closely, the closing of Frida and Diego for the one who still has not made it to MoMA. A list of three names built for that person gets read all the way through. Two hundred and eighty two exhibitions dropped into one message get opened by no one.

That kicks off the busiest week on the calendar. Tomorrow we pick up whatever Danh Vo's preview leaves behind.
