Nineteen thirteen. That's the year Constantin Brancusi finished, in Paris, a bronze head that would take more than a century to find its real price. Good morning on this Thursday, Virginia, just three months ago that sculpture, called Danaide, sold at Christie's New York for one hundred and seven million six hundred thousand dollars, a new all time record for the artist, fifty one percent above the previous one. It came from the collection of media executive Samuel Irving Newhouse, who had bought it at the same house back in two thousand and two for just eighteen million two hundred thousand dollars. We'll come back to that number in a minute, because it says something useful for any client who collects sculpture.

First, the agenda for these days. Sim Chi Yin, the photographer whose work follows Chinese migration out of Malaya in the middle of the last century, closes this Saturday at Storefront for Art and Architecture, so there are only two real days left to see it. Botero in New York stays open at Sotheby's Breuer building through September the seventh, with part of the works also available for private sale, and MoMA keeps its show pairing Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera on view through September the twelfth. At the Whitney, the retrospective devoted to painter Minnie Evans, the museum's first in fifty years, runs through January. And September is turning out busier than it looked. Beyond Danh Vo's return to White Cube on the ninth, this week brought news that two new galleries are opening together in Chelsea for Armory week. Gazelli Art House, until now based only in London and Baku, bought the former Marlborough Gallery space on West Twenty Fifth Street for seven and a half million dollars, a huge space with ceilings close to twenty feet, and opens on September the twenty third. Right across the street, on the same block, the new gallery from Victoria Mouraux Durand-Ruel, a descendant of the legendary Impressionist dealer Paul Durand-Ruel, opens with an inaugural show devoted to French photographer Lucien Clergue. Two openings at once, on the same block, right before the fair itself starts on the twenty fourth.

On the market side, beyond the Brancusi record, the other story of the week is that Sotheby's officially confirmed it secured the Argentine Blaquier family's collection for its November sale, more than four hundred and fifty million dollars across Van Gogh, Cézanne, Degas, Renoir, and Pissarro. The customs dispute that had held up a dozen of those works leaving Argentina was settled last month, when authorities waived the penalty they had proposed against the family, which cleared the rest of the consignment. Back to Brancusi, here's the math worth sharing with any client. Newhouse paid eighteen million two hundred thousand dollars for that sculpture in two thousand and two, and his family just sold it for almost six times that number, twenty four years later. No serious advisor would promise that kind of jump ahead of time, but it's a useful example for a client with modern sculpture in their collection, that the category, not only painting, can also move big numbers when the name and the provenance are solid.

Today's move, tell two or three clients who follow new galleries or collect photography that the two Chelsea openings, Gazelli Art House and the Mouraux Durand-Ruel gallery, land right before Armory, on the very same block on Twenty Fifth Street, with the Lucien Clergue show as a concrete reason to stop by before the fair fills up everyone's calendar.
