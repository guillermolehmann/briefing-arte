What happens when an auction house moves into a museum? Sotheby's has been settled for months now in the Breuer building on Madison Avenue, the structure that was for decades the Whitney's home and later the Met's uptown outpost, and this week's first-half numbers confirm the move is working out better than expected. Good morning, Virginia, it's Tuesday, the twenty fifth of August, and the first result I want to tell you about ties directly to that house.

Sotheby's closed the first six months of the year with four billion four hundred million dollars in total sales, the highest half year in its entire history, a jump of fifty eight percent over the same period last year, driven by marquee lots and by the luxury category. What stands out even more than that big number is private sales, the deals closed in direct conversation that never touch a saleroom, which climbed fifty two percent to eight hundred and twenty six million dollars, also an all-time record, and growing even faster than the public side of the business. Part of that energy comes straight from the new building, since the house reported more than double the visitors in the first half of this year compared with the same stretch of two thousand twenty five at its former home. Inside that same building, Botero in New York is still on view, the show bringing together more than twenty paintings and sculptures from Fernando Botero's New York period, lent by his foundation and by private collectors, open through the seventh of September.

The rest of the agenda is moving against dates that keep getting closer. Sim Chi Yin, the photographer whose installation at Storefront for Art and Architecture deals with the mass deportation of the Chinese community from Malaysia in the middle of last century, closes this coming Saturday, so four days remain. MoMA continues with Frida and Diego, The Last Dream, the show bringing together Frida Kahlo and Diego Rivera, staged alongside a new production at the Met Opera, running through the twelfth of September. At the Whitney, the retrospective that opened yesterday for Minnie Evans, the museum's first in fifty years dedicated to her, with more than one hundred drawings and paintings organized together with the High Museum in Atlanta, stays up through January. As a smaller but real piece of news, Gallery Henoch in Chelsea opened last week a show of Alexandra Pacula, nighttime cityscapes painted in thick layers of oil, on view through mid September.

September is filling up fast. Danh Vo, the artist whose major retrospective ran at the Guggenheim in two thousand eighteen, returns to White Cube with a preview on Wednesday evening the ninth and a public opening the next day, and that date is now less than two weeks away.
