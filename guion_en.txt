Nineteen oh seven. That's the year Vasily Kandinsky painted the largest canvas he had made up to that point, about four feet by five feet, a crowded scene of riders and loose figures. It hung for years in the home of a Jewish family in Amsterdam, until the Nazis occupied the Netherlands and the painting was forced onto the auction block in nineteen forty. From there it went to a German museum, where it stayed on view for decades as if nothing had happened. Only in twenty twenty three, after a six year legal fight, did the German body that reviews these cases side with the family's heirs and order the painting returned.

That Kandinsky painting we opened with is now heading back to market. Christie's is selling it in London on the fourteenth of October, during Frieze week, with an estimate above thirty million dollars, an unusual case of a work only coming up for sale after the question of rightful ownership got settled. That same question, what can leave a country and what can't, is playing out this week on the other side of the ocean with the Blaquier collection. Marcelo Pacheco, who spent eleven years running curatorial affairs at the Malba, the Latin American art museum in Buenos Aires, warned this week that the sale of the collection through Sotheby's takes two thousand pieces of Argentine art out of the country, classic painters from Pueyrredón to Berni, more than thirty paintings by Pettoruti among them. The Blaquier family answered back that very day, in the same newspaper, saying the Argentine and Latin American works in the collection remain in the country and remain off the market, and that what Sotheby's is selling in November is the already known European group, the Van Gogh, the Cézanne, the Degas, the Renoir, and the Pissarro. The argument is still open, but for a client who collects Argentine art the clarification matters, the part of the collection he actually cares about isn't going anywhere.

Today's move is simple. For the clients on the Argentine side who have been following the Blaquier consignment since August, send them the family's clarification before they hear it elsewhere, that what comes up for sale in November is the European collection and that the Argentine and Latin American pieces are staying put. It's a concrete fact that matters to them directly, and better they hear it from you.
