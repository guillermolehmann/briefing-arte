Good Monday morning, Virginia, it is the twenty fourth of August. The week opens with a fresh show at the Whitney, the museum's first retrospective for painter Minnie Evans in fifty years, since the show it organized for her back in nineteen seventy five. Evans was a self-taught Afro-Caribbean artist from North Carolina who started drawing at forty three, after her grandmother died, drawing on her own visions and dreams. It is more than a hundred drawings, paintings, and collages made between nineteen thirty five and nineteen eighty one, organized by the High Museum in Atlanta, and it stays up at the Whitney through January.

The rest of the calendar holds steady. The Whitney Biennial's fifth floor already closed yesterday, Sunday, though the first floor stays up through September the seventh and the sixth floor through October the twelfth. Botero in New York, the show of more than twenty works from the Colombian painter's New York period, stays open at Sotheby's Breuer building through September the seventh. Photographer Sim Chi Yin's installation at Storefront for Art and Architecture, the architecture gallery down in Soho, enters its final week, closing on the twenty ninth. And MoMA holds Frida and Diego, The Last Dream, on Kahlo and Rivera, through September the twelfth.

The Blaquier consignment, the Argentine family's collection that Sotheby's locked in this month for its November sale in New York, finally showed its full face this week. You already knew the Van Gogh, a chestnut tree in blossom from eighteen ninety, comes estimated between one hundred and fifty and two hundred million dollars, and that a Cézanne harlequin could top one hundred and twenty million. What is new is that the rest of the lot now has numbers too. Each of the Degas, Renoir, and Pissarro pieces the family put into the same consignment comes calculated above twenty five million dollars apiece. Added up, the full package remains valued at more than four hundred and fifty million, and now you have five names with their own figure instead of two, which is exactly what you will need if a client asks about the real scale of this sale.

Mondays mean training. Sotheby's Institute of Art has enrollment open for Introduction to Art and Finance, a six week online, asynchronous course, with a cohort running from October the seventh through November the eighteenth, one thousand five hundred and ninety dollars, ending in a Certificate of Completion. It works through the vocabulary that carries a valuation conversation with a client, art-secured lending, price indices, art treated as an asset class. The catch is timing, the cohort wraps right as New York's November season kicks off, so it means splitting time between studying and Virginia's own evening sales.

Today's move is to let the two or three clients you have been keeping posted on the Blaquier consignment know that estimates are now out for all five works, not just the Van Gogh and the Cézanne, and that those five pieces together already add up to well over half the collection's total valuation. It is a concrete excuse to reconnect before the season kicks off, and it also gives you a precise figure to explain why this particular consignment is getting so much attention.
