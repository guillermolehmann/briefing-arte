Quick review. Yesterday the client who didn't want to step into a room full of people asked you about the private sale, what did you explain to him about the discretion and the time it can take, and what was the one thing you couldn't promise him?... That it exists, that it's built around each client, but that it doesn't speed up the evaluation of the work or guarantee a better price than a room with real competition. And from a few days before that, when the client had doubts about a certificate from a small gallery, what document did you tell her brings together the work of an artist's estate specialists to confirm a work is genuine?... The catalogue raisonné.

The same client from yesterday, now set on selling through the private route, asks you the question that was left hanging. The work has to leave his apartment in Manhattan on its way to the buyer, and he wants to know who takes care of moving it, and whether that deal pays the same New York taxes it would if Phillips had sold it under the hammer in a room full of people.

Let's start with the tax, since that's what confuses people most. Picture two identical buyers, both buying the same work in the same Manhattan saleroom, for the same price. The first one takes it home that same afternoon, three blocks away. The second one asks for it to be shipped directly, with a full bill of lading, to his house in Miami, so the work never passes through his hands in New York. The first one pays New York sales tax on the purchase price. The second one, if that Florida delivery is properly documented, can end up owing New York nothing, though he may end up owing Florida tax instead. What decides it isn't where the buyer lives or what passport he carries, it's the place where the house actually hands over the work, which is why it's known as the destination rule. Never promise a client that buying in New York comes tax free just because he lives somewhere else.

Let's run the numbers for real. In Manhattan, the combined sales tax rate is eight point eight seven five percent, made up of four percent for New York State, four and a half percent for the city, and a small additional slice for the metro transportation district. On a hundred thousand dollar purchase delivered right there in Manhattan, that's eight thousand eight hundred and seventy five dollars in tax, on top of the purchase price. If instead the house ships the work straight to another state and can prove that delivery with the right paperwork, New York can end up outside the deal entirely, though the destination state may have its own tax and can even require the house itself to collect it on its behalf. And watch for a detail people forget, if that work later comes back into New York for the buyer's own use, a different tax can show up, use tax, which follows the work even when the original purchase escaped sales tax.

The same confusion shows up with freeports, those high security warehouses where many collectors store expensive pieces near ports or airports, under a special customs regime that can defer duty payments while the work stays under customs control. A client may show up thinking that putting a work in a freeport makes it automatically tax free forever. That's the moment to stop him, because the customs regime can defer a payment while the work sits inside, but the treatment changes the moment that work enters domestic consumption, gets sold, or gets reexported, and hiding who the real owner is or lying about a piece's value to dodge taxes stops being planning and becomes a legal problem outright.

With all that in hand, you need to be careful about what you claim. Shipping a work to another state doesn't come tax free automatically, it depends on the delivery being properly documented and on what rules that other state has. Storing it in a freeport doesn't make the deal tax free either, it depends on the exact regime and on what happens to the work afterward. And if the client starts talking about art as an investment, that's where you stop him, because the market's own track record doesn't back up that expectation, over twenty years the index of the hundred most important artworks returned just a bit over three percent a year, well below what the most widely followed U.S. stock index returned over the same period. What is real is that art backed lending grew fast, and is now measured in the tens of billions of dollars in outstanding loans, so there's a real option today of using a collection as loan collateral, which is a very different thing from expecting that collection to rise in price like a stock.

You can now answer your client with precision. A specialized fine art shipper or the house's own logistics department handles moving the work, insurance included, and that cost falls on the buyer or gets negotiated separately depending on the deal. New York tax depends on where the final delivery is documented, not on where he lives or how quickly he wants it out of the state, so for a deal this size it's worth working out the logistics with the house's own tax team before the work moves an inch. And if at some point he asks whether it makes sense to buy thinking about resale as if it were a financial asset, answer him with the same index figure, that over the long run art hasn't beaten the stock market, and what does make sense today is the option of borrowing against his own collection, a real and growing financial use, different from expecting a capital gain.

Think about this for tomorrow. If that same client already has the work pledged as collateral on a bank loan, can he still consign it for a private sale, or does the bank get a say first? That's the question the week's exam opens with.
