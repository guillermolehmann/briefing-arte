Three days go by. That same collector writes to you, says she's still thinking about that sentence, and asks to schedule a video call this week. You've already built the brief from what she let slip at the Armory, something in warm color, something that reminds her of family gatherings, nothing in the way of formal portraits. In that stretch you have to say why this painting, why at this price, and why now, because her husband joins halfway through the call and doesn't speak Spanish.

Look at the difference between two ways of showing the same work. One person describes it, gives the artist's name, the size, the color, and waits for the client to decide alone whether she likes it. The other person builds an argument, tells where the work comes from, shows what the market has recently paid for comparable pieces, and explains why this is the moment and not just any moment. The first sells an object. The second sells an informed decision, and that difference is what separates a yes from an uncomfortable silence. This is called the two minute pitch, and it has three fixed parts, always in the same order. History, where the work comes from and why it exists. Comparables, what real people have recently paid for something similar, to anchor the price in something other than your own word. And why now, the concrete signal that makes waiting more costly than deciding today, a show, a retrospective, a recent record. The order matters as much as the content, because if you open with the price it sounds like a sale and the client gets defensive, while if you open with the story the client leans in before knowing the number, and by the time it arrives she already wants it to work. Without that third part, besides, the client can always say she'll think about it, because nothing is pressing her.

For today's example we're using Adriana Varejão, a Brazilian painter who works with glazed ceramic and paint on motifs that reference Brazil's colonial history, tiles that seem to crack open and reveal something underneath. History, she's spent more than three decades building that idea, the surface that hides a wound. Comparable, this past June a round piece of hers, a monochrome, sold at Phillips London for three hundred and seventy four thousand one hundred dollars, well below her historic record set more than a decade ago. I picked that number instead of the record on purpose, because a comparable's job isn't to impress, it's to describe what a real buyer paid recently for something similar, and a ten year old record says nothing about what the market is willing to pay today. Why now, this year Varejão is representing Brazil at the Venice Biennale, the most important international art exhibition on the calendar, the kind of platform where an important curator picks very few artists at a time.

With those three pieces you build the pitch in Spanish. Adriana Varejão lleva treinta años pintando la historia de Brasil debajo de la superficie de un azulejo. En junio, en Londres, una pieza suya de este tipo se vendió por trescientos setenta y cuatro mil cien dólares. Y este año Brasil la eligió para representarlo en la Bienal de Venecia, así que el mundo la está mirando justo ahora.

And here's the short English version, for when the husband joins. Adriana Varejão has spent three decades painting Brazil's history underneath the surface of a cracked tile. In June, in London, a piece like this one sold for three hundred and seventy four thousand one hundred dollars. And this year Brazil chose her to represent the country at the Venice Biennale, so the art world is watching her right now.

What you can't say is that the Biennale is going to raise the price of this specific piece. Representing a country in Venice is verifiable prestige, not a market promise, and a single recent result is a data point, not a confirmed trend. Look at the contrast with Doris Salcedo, a Colombian sculptor known for installations about disappearance and violence. The Whitechapel Gallery in London named her Art Icon in twenty twenty five, one of the highest institutional honors that exists, and yet her auction record remains far below Varejão's, with very few public sales on record behind it. Institutional prestige and auction price don't always rise together or at the same pace, and that has to be said plainly before the client assumes it on her own.

With the collector, if she asks whether this piece will be worth more in five years, you can tell her what's true, that there's fresh institutional attention and a real recent price anchoring the conversation, and what you can't promise her, that the prestige will translate into price within any set timeline. Something like this works. "I can't guarantee you a number in five years, but I can tell you that right now the circuit that decides which artist matters is watching her, and that the last real price someone paid for something similar was this one." That's enough for her to decide with information, not with a guarantee you have no way of backing up, and it also keeps you on the right side of things if the price doesn't move the way she expected.
