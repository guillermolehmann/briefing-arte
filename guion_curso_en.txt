Week four, lesson four.

Yesterday you built the two minute pitch for Adriana Varejão. What were the three parts, in the right order?... History, comparables, and why now, always in that order, because opening with the price puts the client on the defensive.

The collector from the Armory Show already heard that full pitch. She loved the work, and told you, in the same words from yesterday, that it feels expensive. This is the first time she has said it to your face, right in the middle of the call, and you need an answer now, not after you hang up and think it over.

The first thing to understand is that feels expensive works as a request for proof dressed up as an objection, and three more show up just as often in a real sales conversation, what if it is fake, what if it is worth less later, and let me ask my advisor, each one asking for its own kind of proof. Answering all four with the same generic reassurance, the trust me kind, is the fastest way to sink a deal that was going well. The one about price wants a real number to measure against, the one about authenticity wants paperwork, the one about future value wants historical data, and the one that mentions an advisor simply wants information to carry along.

Start with price, since that is the one facing you right now. When someone says a work is expensive, they are almost never arguing with the number itself, they are asking whether that number matches what the market is actually paying today for something comparable, and that question gets answered with data, not with a speech about the artist's talent. Go back to the comparables file you built for yesterday's pitch, the Varejão piece that sold at Phillips London in June for three hundred and seventy four thousand one hundred dollars, and add a second figure you have not shared yet, another round monochrome from the same series that Phillips New York sold on May twenty first for three hundred and sixty one thousand two hundred dollars. Two houses, two cities, two different months, and both results land in the same price band. That is what turns a single number into a pattern, and a pattern carries more weight than any dealer's opinion, yours included.

With both figures on the table, the conversation shifts on its own. You are no longer defending your price against her judgment, you are both looking together at what real buyers paid weeks ago for something comparable, and from there it is her call whether she wants to pay in that range. That does not guarantee a yes, but it moves you out of the uncomfortable spot of asking her to trust your word alone.

The second objection, what if it is fake, gets answered with documentation, the chain of provenance and the artist's catalogue raisonné when one exists, the same idea you already worked through with the painting attributed to Tarsila do Amaral that was challenged at a São Paulo fair. The third, what if it is worth less later, gets answered with a finding from a Bank of America and ArtTactic study of the art market, that works held for twenty to twenty five years returned an average of eleven percent a year, while those sold before the five year mark lost an average of five point seven percent a year. None of the three numbers you just used, not the price comparable, not the provenance, not the historical return, lets you promise anything about this specific work. They let you show real evidence, and evidence is not the same thing as a guarantee, so say that plainly whenever you use them. And watch the date on whichever comparable you pick, because a result from fifteen years ago is good for telling the artist's story, and justifying a current price takes a more recent one. The same goes for documentation, a complete provenance file lowers the risk of standing in front of a fake considerably, but on high value pieces the final word still belongs to an authentication specialist, not to you or the file in your hand.

The fourth, let me ask my advisor, is the easiest one to ruin out of anxiety. Treat it as an opening, because an advisor holding real numbers closes faster than one who only gets a secondhand burst of enthusiasm. Send the collector the same file you used on the call, both comparables with their date and house, so her advisor can review numbers instead of just a story.

For today's client, the full answer comes together like this. I understand it feels expensive, and that is why I am sending you right now the two results to use as your reference, the Phillips London one from June and the Phillips New York one from May, so you can decide with those numbers in front of you and not just my word. On authenticity, if she asks, this piece's documented provenance is complete and you can walk her through all of it. On future value, you cannot promise a number, but the record shows long holds outperforming quick resales, and that is the logic behind buying this. And if she wants to check with her advisor, good, let that advisor see the same file you are holding.

The collector says she will think about the price, and before hanging up she mentions something else, almost in passing, a small inherited work sitting in her house that she might sell someday. That opens the other half of this lesson, because asking for a consignment turns out to work a lot like answering an objection. Do not open with a number, open by asking what she actually knows about the piece, and offer her the same thing you just gave her as a buyer, a real file of comparables for that artist, so she can decide to move forward with information instead of the discomfort of feeling rushed.
