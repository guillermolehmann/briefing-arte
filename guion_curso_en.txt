Week three, practice. Today's assignment is a collector who wants to sell her Jesús Rafael Soto sculpture and wants to understand the whole process before signing anything.

Her name is Marisol, she's Venezuelan, she's lived in Miami for fifteen years, and she inherited from her parents a metal sculpture with moving panels, the kind of work that made Soto famous. She has no purchase invoice, nothing beyond an old family photo of the piece hanging in the family home in Caracas. She asks you, in this order, whether it's worth selling, how much she can get, and what she needs to do first.

From this week you already have the five layers to work through before answering her with any precision, and today you apply them one by one to a real case instead of hearing them in the abstract.

First, the physical layer. Before talking about a dollar figure, someone has to see the piece in person, or at least close technical photographs, to confirm the panels move the way they should, that none is missing, and that the structure has no rust or damage requiring restoration. That's what a condition report does, and without one no specialist puts a serious estimate on the table. And as you saw in this week's first lesson, it's worth asking Marisol whether it's a unique piece or part of an edition, because Soto's kinetic sculptures circulated both ways, and that single answer already moves the price ceiling before you look at anything else.

Second, the documentary layer, the weakest part of Marisol's case. She has no invoice, no certificate, only the family photo. That doesn't rule the work out, but it means rebuilding provenance from scratch, who her parents bought it from if she remembers, at what gallery or fair, and whether any archive or foundation tracking Soto's work can check the piece against a registry. Without that groundwork, no major house will accept it for a top tier sale.

Third, the contractual layer, the consignment itself. You already saw this week that Sotheby's is the only one of the big three that publishes a standard seller's commission, ten percent of the hammer price, with a five hundred dollar minimum and an extra two percent success fee when the result beats the agreed high estimate, while Christie's and Phillips negotiate case by case. You also saw that guarantees exist, a secured price floor set before the sale, offered by the house itself or shared with a third party. For a piece like Marisol's, without the profile of a nine figure lot, it's unlikely any house offers a guarantee, so the real conversation runs through the reserve, the minimum price below which the work doesn't sell, not through any secured floor.

Fourth, the channel layer, and here's the most important fact in this case. Soto's auction record is still The Hacksaw, sold at Sotheby's New York on November the nineteenth, two thousand twelve, for one million eighty two thousand five hundred dollars, and the public market sources available today don't document with any solidity how his sculptures and objects have moved between two thousand twenty three and now. That gap in evidence doesn't mean Soto's market is dead, it means any estimate for Marisol's piece has to lean on specific comparables a specialist tracks down case by case, not on a number someone remembers off the top of their head. And with a smaller pool of buyers for kinetic sculpture than for painting of the same period, following the criterion you already saw this week about when public auction makes sense and when a private sale does, it's worth weighing both channels before picking one.

Fifth, the final layer, the smallest one here but still real. If Marisol sells in New York and the house delivers the work there, the city's combined tax applies, eight point eight seven five percent of the purchase price. Since she's the seller and not the buyer, she doesn't pay that tax out of pocket, but she should know it becomes part of the price conversation with whoever ends up buying, and that it can change depending on where delivery is documented.

What you can't tell Marisol is exactly what her sculpture is worth based on the two thousand twelve record. That number shows Soto can reach seven figures when the piece and the buyer line up, not what someone would pay today for a work without documented provenance or recent resale evidence. You also can't promise her that a public auction will get her a better price than a private sale, because that depends on how many real buyers exist for that specific piece, and you don't know that yet.

What you can tell Marisol is the full process, in order. First, a specialist has to see the work in person or with serious technical photographs and put together the condition report, confirming along the way whether it's unique or an edition. Second, rebuild the provenance as completely as possible, even with family testimony and photos, before discussing any number. Third, only once those two steps are done does it make sense to ask one or two houses for a preliminary estimate, and compare how each frames commission, reserve, and channel, public auction against private sale. What you need to find out before the next call is whether an active Soto foundation or archive exists that can help confirm the piece, and where you need to stay careful is with any figure you give her before that work is done.

Marisol listens to all of this and says something you didn't expect, that she's actually in no rush to sell, that what matters most to her is that the work ends up in a collection or a museum likely to show it, and that the money is beside the point. That comment opens tomorrow's class, which starts week four, because not every collector and not every seller moves for the same reason, and tomorrow you'll learn to tell them apart before they spell it out for you.
