Week one, practice. Selling Art in New York.

Before we start, a question about yesterday's review. In the scene with the collector from Bogotá, do you remember why Phillips doesn't compete number for number with Christie's and Sotheby's, even though all three work the auction side of the business... Because Phillips plays the selection game instead of the volume game, betting on artists whose market is still taking shape.

That same collector writes to you today. With just over a month to go before you walk the Armory Show floor together, in person she asks for a dry run before the real day, over video call, looking at the exhibitor list the fair just published. This year the fair runs from the twenty fourth to the twenty seventh of September at the Javits Center. You say yes, and that is where today's practice begins, the same one you will have to perform in person in a few weeks, with a real client instead of a rehearsal on screen.

The first thing you show her is that a big fair gets prepared days ahead. Galleries send out PDFs in advance showing what they are bringing, and the most sought after work tends to sell early, sometimes on the very first day, before the general public even gets in the door. That is why it pays to go through those PDFs ahead of time, build a short list of two or three priorities with the client, and only then leave room for open exploration. Esthella Provas, an art advisor who has worked the big Miami fairs for years, puts it simply, she reads the PDFs before traveling, takes notes, and arrives at the fair with priorities already discussed with the client. With your collector you do exactly that, you go through the exhibitor list together, mark which galleries carry the Latin American artists she already cares about, and build the first day's route around those booths.

But a good list isn't enough if you can't listen once you're actually there. That is the other half of the practice, the part that separates a real guide from someone just running through an itinerary. Carola Wiese, an art advisor in UBS's family advisory practice, says her job is to stay a step behind, listen first, and only then suggest, without putting her own taste ahead of the person standing next to her. That's the exception that puts a limit on the priority list, because if your collector stops in front of a work that wasn't on the plan and her eyes light up, that moment is worth more than any itinerary drawn up the night before. The list saves you time, listening tells you when to drop it.

And there's something neither the list nor the listening solves on its own, which is not confusing enthusiasm with an obligation to weigh in on price before you have anything to compare it to. If your collector falls for a work at the first booth, the sound move is to ask when it was made, what other fairs that gallery has shown work by the same artist at, and whether there are recent comparables before saying whether the price is fair. Genuine enthusiasm doesn't give you that information, and stating something you don't actually know yet can cost you trust down the road. Saying you'll find out and come back with an answer the next day is worth more, in the long run, than improvising a number in front of the dealer just to avoid staying quiet.

There's still what happens after the lights go down in the hall, because that is where many advisors lose what they built inside. Evan Tawil, an independent art advisor who builds much of his client base on referrals, says his reputation rests on responding fast and being transparent about what he can and can't deliver, instead of leaving conversations hanging with no word back. With your collector that means writing to her the next day, not the following week, telling her what follow up you did with the galleries she liked, even if the follow up is simply that there's no news yet. And if you shook hands on a deal at some booth, don't reopen it later trying to sweeten it, because trying to renegotiate something already agreed is one of the fastest ways to break trust between two parties. You close a fair well when the relationship with the client and with the galleries is better than it was before you walked in.

In today's rehearsal, your collector asks you a question she is sure to ask again in person. She tells you she saw a work at one booth that she loved but doesn't know if the price is reasonable, and asks you point blank what you think. There's no hammer price involved here because it's the primary market, a fixed price set by the gallery, so your answer can't lean on an estimate or a reserve, it has to lean on what you actually know about that artist in the primary market, and if you don't know, on telling her you'll find out before committing to an opinion. Practice that answer out loud before the video call, because it's the one you'll get asked most often.
