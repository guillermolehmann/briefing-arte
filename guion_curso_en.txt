Quick review. Yesterday the certificate client asked you how the process continues now that the second opinion confirmed the piece, what did you explain to him about the reserve, the commission and the guarantee before Phillips evaluates the work up close?... That the reserve is the agreed minimum price, that the seller's commission depends on the house and the value of the piece, and that if Phillips considers it strong enough it can offer him a guarantee that takes the risk off his shoulders, but that none of those three terms gets settled over the phone with you, the specialist department decides them after examining the work in person.

The new client from yesterday's close, the one who doesn't want his work stepping onto the stage of a saleroom full of people after the doubt that circulated about the gallery that certified it, calls you again. He doesn't want to back out of selling, he just wants to do it away from prying eyes, and he asks whether that really exists, a way to sell an expensive work without going through the public block of an auction.

It exists, and all three major houses have had it built into their own structure for years, a private sales department. The work never climbs onto an auctioneer's stage, never appears printed in a catalogue open to anyone, and the price gets negotiated in direct conversation between the house, the seller and a buyer who's either already identified or one the house goes out to find specifically for that piece. That deal is called a private sale.

Putting together a private sale starts almost backwards from a consignment for auction, and it also runs on a different clock. A public auction has a fixed date, with months of lead time to build the catalogue and a single night for the buyer to show up. A private sale waits on no calendar at all, it can close next week or it can take a full year of searching, depending on how long it takes the right counterpart to appear. Instead of announcing the work to the whole market and waiting for interest to surface, the house first builds a precise brief, the artist, the series, the year, the size, even the color, and from there activates its internal network of relationship managers spread around the world to track down exactly that piece or exactly that buyer. David Schrader, who runs Sotheby's private sales division, has said that some clients ask that only two people know the full details of the deal from start to finish, while others insist outright that the work must never set foot inside a Sotheby's building, which means the viewing gets arranged in a warehouse or at the buyer's own home. That same discretion also protects the seller from another risk, that of a work going to auction, failing to reach its reserve, and ending up on the public record as a piece that found no buyer that night. A private negotiation that never closes simply leaves no trace at all.

That level of discretion doesn't slow the business down, it speeds it up. In the first half of this year, while Sotheby's closed the strongest half year in its entire history with four billion four hundred million dollars in total sales, private sales grew even faster than the public side, fifty two percent, to eight hundred and twenty six million dollars, also an all-time record for the house. On the other side of the table, a buyer might prefer the same route for a similar reason, avoiding word getting out about what they just added to their collection before they're ready to show it off.

Careful, none of this has anything to do with bidding by phone or through an online platform during a public auction. Those two are simply ways of taking part from a distance in the very same saleroom, with the same hammer and the same catalogue open to anyone who wants to look. A private sale is a different thing entirely, it's the complete absence of that room.

What a private sale doesn't guarantee you is a higher price. Without public bidding there aren't two buyers heating each other up over the same work, which is exactly what can push an auction result well above estimate. It doesn't save you the serious evaluation of the piece either, because before going out to find a buyer the house still needs to put a real number on it, and that number has to come from a professional appraisal, not from eyeballing what something similar sold for last year. An appraisal and an auction estimate are not the same thing, even though people mix them up constantly. The auction estimate is a figure built for that one sale, meant to draw bidding on the night, and it's usually set by the very house that will later collect commission on the result. An appraisal is different, done by an independent appraiser who isn't paid a cut of the work's value but a flat fee for the job, following a recognized professional standard, and it serves an insurance policy, an estate, or dividing an inheritance among siblings, without it benefiting anyone to push the number up or down.

You can already answer your new client. Tell him yes, a route without a public saleroom or a catalogue does exist, with the discretion he's asking for, and that it also doesn't have to wait for the next auction date to start, the search for a buyer can begin next week. Make just as clear, in that same conversation, that this route doesn't speed up a serious evaluation of the work or guarantee him a better number than a room full of real competition would, that the final result still depends on finding the right buyer rather than on public exposure, and that if at some point he needs a number for insurance or for dividing an inheritance among siblings he's going to need a separate appraisal too, done by his own professional, not the number the house gives him to go sell the piece. What you can't tell him yet is how long it will take for that buyer to show up, because unlike an auction with a fixed date, a private sale can close in a week or can keep searching for months after.
