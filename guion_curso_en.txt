Week two, lesson one. Selling Art in New York.

Before we start, a question about yesterday's practice session. In the rehearsal with the collector from Bogotá, when she asked you whether the price on a piece at a booth was reasonable... what did you need to lean on to answer, since there was no hammer price or reserve involved at all? Comparables for that specific artist in the primary market, and if you did not have them on hand, telling her you would find out before committing to an opinion.

A client writes to you the moment she hears that Sotheby's landed the Blaquier family collection, one of Argentina's largest, for its November sale in New York. She sends you the artists' names, Van Gogh, Cézanne, Degas, and asks why a house builds an entire evening sale around those particular names and not others just as well known within twentieth century art. You answer with what you are about to learn today.

When a family decides to sell an entire collection, houses compete hard to land it, and that competition does not spread evenly across every painting in the group. A painting by a name with decades of auction history, with dozens of prior sales to compare on size, period and condition, gives the house something it can sell with confidence, guaranteed press coverage on sale night, and buyers who already know what they are looking at. That painting works as an anchor, pulling the rest of the collection along behind it, including pieces by less recognized artists that would never fill a saleroom on their own. Picasso and Matisse are, among the moderns, the names that play that role most often, because there is barely a decade since nineteen hundred without a sale of one or the other, which gives any specialist an enormous track record to lean an estimate on.

That logic was on full display this past May, when Sotheby's brought in three hundred and three million nine hundred thousand dollars in modern art in a single night. The most contested piece was a Matisse, La Chaise lorraine, which had not been seen on the market in nearly fifty years, coming out of a private Swiss collection, estimated at twenty five million dollars. Bidding stretched past ten minutes and closed at forty eight million four hundred thousand dollars, the second highest price ever paid for a work by the artist. That same night, a Picasso, a bust of a harlequin, sold for forty two million six hundred thousand dollars. With those two names carrying much of the room's attention, the full sale closed with ninety eight percent of the lots sold, a very high figure that shows just how much demand stood behind those two signatures.

That same anchor logic shows up outside the moderns too. Last November, Sotheby's built an entire surrealist art sale around a single Frida Kahlo painting, El sueño, La cama, which went for fifty four million six hundred and sixty thousand dollars and stood as the highest price ever paid for a work by a woman artist. That painting did not just make history on its own, it pulled attention toward the rest of the sale, with works by other surrealists and other Latin American names selling better than they would have on their own that same night.

Now, watch out for the easy conclusion. Kahlo setting a record like that does not mean every piece of Latin American surrealist art went up in price that night, nor that any name from the region is worth more just for sitting near her in the catalogue. Kahlo's record sits behind an exceedingly rare painting of exceptional quality, and something similar holds for Leonora Carrington, the Anglo-Irish Surrealist painter who spent much of her working life in Mexico, who kept selling seven figure paintings in the years after her own two thousand twenty four record, confirming there was real depth behind the headline. Something similar happens with Diego Rivera, the Mexican muralist who was married to Kahlo, who holds a high record from years back, but recent sales comparable to each other are few, so it is worth keeping the price of one exceptional masterwork separate from the price of what usually circulates by that same artist. For several other names in the same movement, though, there still are not enough recent public sales that compare well with each other to say the interest holds evenly across the board, so an isolated record says a great deal about that particular painting and very little about the rest of the market.

With a client, that turns into a very concrete question before you comment on any consignment that comes to you attached to a big name. If someone brings you a painting by an artist who shares a category with Picasso or with Kahlo but does not have the same track record, ask first how many recent sales exist for that specific artist, not for the category as a whole. You can put it this way, I understand you are excited that this sits in the same family as that record, but before we talk price I need to see that artist's own track record, because the anchor pulls the rest of the sale along, it does not lend its price directly to every single piece. If the client pushes for a number that same afternoon, offer to meet the next day once you have looked at the comparables, rather than improvising a figure you would later have to walk back in public.

That same client is going to write to you again once she hears that the collection Sotheby's landed for November belongs to an Argentine family, the Blaquiers, valued at more than four hundred and fifty million dollars across Van Gogh, Cézanne, Degas, Renoir and Pissarro. She will ask why a collection like that never ends up at Phillips instead of Sotheby's or Christie's. Think through the answer with what you already know from week one about the scale gap between the three houses, and have it ready for the next time someone asks you over dinner.
