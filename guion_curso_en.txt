Week two, closing practice.

You are at a reception before a Phillips preview, glass in hand, and a Mexican client comes up to you, someone who bought a couple of mid-size pieces from you years ago and now wants to grow the collection. She is not asking about some name she heard at a dinner party. She tells you, straight out, that she trusts your judgment more than her previous advisor's, and asks you to help her decide where to put her money within Latin American art.

Before you answer, line up in your head the same criterion you used all week. Any artist can land an auction record, all it takes is one lucky night and two stubborn bidders. The question that actually matters is whether there are repeat sales behind that record, across more than one house and more than one season, or whether the record is the only public data point that exists. It is the same difference between picking a restaurant off one glowing review and picking it because it has been packed every night for months. That distinction is what separates professional advice from a bet with a nice name attached, and it is the same one you applied all week with Botero, with Fordjour, with Amaral.

Think first of Olga de Amaral. She has a fresh record, three million one hundred twenty five thousand dollars at Christie's this past November for a textile piece. What makes her a serious recommendation, beyond that number alone, is that between last May and this March at least six more of her sales show up, spread across several houses, all above six hundred ninety eight thousand dollars. And she is coming off a recent retrospective at the Fondation Cartier in Paris. Record, repetition, and museum, all three at once. That is the name you mention first, with confidence, because all three kinds of evidence line up.

Add a second name among the living, Derek Fordjour, who sold a work for eight hundred eighty nine thousand dollars this past May and another for one million one hundred forty three thousand in November, twenty percent above his previous record at another house. Two sales, two houses, two years, moving in the same direction. That repetition is exactly what is still missing for a name like Firelei Báez, who had a strong result in November and saw it topped that same day by another house, with no second sale since to confirm whether that level holds.

Keep that pair, Fordjour and Báez, as the question you can ask about any name a client brings you, did it show one high result once, or did it repeat across more than one house and more than one year. That single question saves you half the mistakes in this business.

Now think of Gego. An institutional résumé as strong as Amaral's, a traveling retrospective that went through the Guggenheim Bilbao, the Guggenheim in New York, the Jumex, and MASP in São Paulo. But her auction record has stood at one million five hundred twelve thousand five hundred dollars since twenty seventeen, with no recent sales that repeat it or come close. There you have two artists with the same museum-grade prestige and completely different auction markets behind them.

Your client is going to ask you, and fairly so, why you are not recommending Gego with the same conviction if the résumé is just as good. That is exactly where you draw the line for her. A retrospective certifies curatorial interest, it does not predict when the market will follow with price. You can tell her to watch Gego, to follow her closely, even to buy something small if she genuinely likes the work. What you cannot do is promise her it will be worth triple in two years just because it passed through four major museums.

There is a third name carrying a double institutional signal this same season, Fernando Botero. Beyond his twenty twenty three record and the five more seven-figure paintings he sold over the following two years, this season he also has Botero in New York open at Sotheby's and, come October, his first London show in eleven years, at Opera Gallery. Two institutions on two continents looking at the same artist in the same season, on top of a market that was already repeating strong results. That is another name with real depth behind it, not just a museum résumé.

Your client then asks, what if I'm wrong, what if I buy and it doesn't go up. There you tell her nobody can guarantee a rise, and that the real value of recommending this way is being able to explain, house by house and date by date, exactly why each name sits where it sits, instead of answering her with a hunch dressed up as expertise.

So here is how you answer her. Tell her to look at Amaral first, with the record and the repetition already confirmed, to add Fordjour among the contemporaries with that same solidity, and to also watch Botero, reinforced this season by the double institutional signal. Tell her to keep an eye on Gego without rushing to pay as if the price were already about to move. And if someone offers her a young artist promising the work will triple in value in two years, tell her to call you before signing anything, because that kind of promise never comes out of a sales record, it comes out of somebody's hurry to sell.

Your client pauses and tells you that at the same fair she saw two versions of the same established Latin American artist, a large oil and a portfolio of numbered prints, and asks which one you would recommend she suggest to a client of her own who has a lot less to spend.

Before she walks away, she adds one more thing, an acquaintance recently offered her a thirty-year-old painter fresh off a waiting list, promising the work will triple in value in two years. You tell her the same thing you already know, no promise of a quick resale replaces a real sales record, and that painter does not have one yet.

Think about that one yourself too. That's the question next week starts with.

That's today's session. Run it through your head once before you go to sleep, because I'm asking you about it tomorrow.
