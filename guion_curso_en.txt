Week four, final practice. No review today, yesterday's exam already did that job, and today we close out the whole course with the drill we have been building toward since week one, one fair, three clients, three conversations happening at once.

Picture the floor of the Armory Show, at the Javits Center, aisles full, its curated section looking toward the Caribbean and its diaspora this year. You do not have to wait for the real thing to practice this, so let's set up the day as if you were already in there, moving between three separate conversations without losing the thread of any of them.

All three have one thing in common, and it is the only thing really worth holding onto from the four weeks. Each client is asking you, without saying so directly, for a different kind of proof. One wants evidence that the price is real, another wants evidence they will not regret it, and the third wants evidence that the process is legitimate. Your job is to identify which proof is missing in each specific case and hand over exactly that one.

First conversation. It is the Bogotá collector you have been building toward since week one, now with her husband beside her, who does not speak Spanish. She tells you what she told you before, that she wants something that speaks to her. You already have the pitch built around Adriana Varejão, the Brazilian artist known for surfaces that reference the colonial history of Brazil through tile work, with three decades of career behind her. You give it in the usual three parts, story, comparable, and why now, with the why now resting on the fact that Varejão is representing Brazil at this year's Venice Biennale, the most closely watched contemporary art exhibition on the international calendar, and in a short version for the husband. She listens to the whole pitch and tells you it feels expensive. You do not repeat your opinion here, you pull a second comparable, the same artist selling at Phillips New York in May for three hundred sixty one thousand two hundred dollars, landing in the same band as the Phillips London result from June you had already shown her. Two houses, two cities, the same price range, and that carries more weight than any argument of yours. And when she adds that she still wants a second opinion at Christie's before deciding, you do not get defensive. You tell her that sounds right, that it is exactly what any serious buyer would do, and you offer to help her set up that meeting yourself. A strong pitch does not fear comparison, it invites it.

Second conversation, a few steps away. A client you already know, the decorator type, asks you again whether the piece he likes has room to appreciate. You start by listening to what is actually worrying him, whether it is the money or the fear of having made a mistake, before explaining anything about the market. Once it is clear it is the second one, that is when you show him the joint Bank of America and ArtTactic study, eleven percent average annual return on works held between twenty and twenty five years, against negative five point seven percent on those sold before five. And you add the contrast from week two, the painter Derek Fordjour repeated his price across two houses and two different years, while a single high result from another artist is not enough to call it a trend. A market wide figure does not promise anything for that specific piece, but it does ease the anxiety about selling too soon, which is what he was really asking about underneath.

Third conversation, the longest one. Marisol, the Venezuelan collector with the inherited Soto, confirms what she said in week three's practice, that the work's final home matters more to her than any urgency to sell, and asks how the process actually continues from here. That takes you back to the fine print, Sotheby's standard ten percent seller's commission plus a two percent success fee, against the case by case negotiation you would get at Christie's or Phillips, and you explain why a piece without documented provenance like hers would not qualify for a guarantee at any of the three houses. You also go back to the scale gap from week one, because a consignment of this size and family history tends to land at Sotheby's or Christie's rather than Phillips, simply because those two handle that volume of business every year. Marisol does not sign anything today, but she asks you for the draft agreement to read over on her own time.

Three conversations, and none of them close, and that is exactly how it should go. A good day at a fair is not measured in contracts signed on the spot, it is measured in how many of those conversations you can pick back up next week with something concrete in hand. Closing in the moment is not the same as closing well either, the Bogotá couple may still choose Christie's after their second opinion, the decorator client may not buy anything this year, and Marisol may take months to sign. Each of those three outcomes is fine as it stands, because each one stays a live conversation, fed by the proof that client actually asked for instead of the one that would have been easier for you to hand over.

After the fair, your list looks like this. You send the Bogotá couple the two Varejão comparables in writing along with the offer to set up the Christie's meeting. You send the decorator client the holding period return chart, with a line making clear it is a market average and not a guarantee for one specific piece. And you send Marisol the standard consignment agreement from Sotheby's Latin American art department, so she can read it before your next call.

With this the entire course is complete. Week one placed you on the board, the full circuit and where Phillips stands inside it. Week two gave you the canon you need to recognize without hesitating, from the European moderns to the established names of Latin American art. Week three took you inside the mechanics, consignment, provenance, and the fine print you used with Marisol today. And this week four brought you back to the client, the four types, the objections, and finally yourself as part of what gets sold. Today's three conversations did not use a single new tool, they used all four weeks together, at the same time, which is exactly how they get used in real life.
