The reserve is the confidential minimum price the consignor accepts, and the estimate is the public range the house calculates to attract bids, the reserve almost always lands somewhere inside or near the low estimate. Today's takeaway is one thing, the primary market and the secondary market are two different games, and the megagalleries already play both at once.

First movement. The primary market is the direct sale, the gallery represents a living artist, sets the price, and decides who gets the work. The secondary market is resale, anything that's already changed hands at least once, that's where auctions show up, but so do the same galleries, buying and reselling secondhand work. The Art Basel and UBS report on 2025 measured the real size of each, dealers and galleries generated thirty four billion, eight hundred million dollars, almost sixty percent of the entire art market, while public and private auctions combined landed well below that, around twenty five billion. A concrete case of how that line moves, the gallery Perrotin built a separate unit a few years back called Second Marché, dedicated entirely to the secondary market. Its directors explained at an industry talk that they created it after noticing that even major collectors got split attention, one person sold them new work and someone else entirely bought or resold the older pieces. Now the same gallery can be there for a client on both ends, when they want to buy and when they want to sell.

So far, then, primary is straight from the artist, secondary is resale, and the big galleries no longer choose between the two businesses, they build a leg for each.

Second movement, the fairs. They're mostly the showcase of the primary market. Here's the calendar that matters to you. The Armory Show in New York runs September twenty fourth through twenty seventh, at the Javits Center, the semester's first big date after the August lull. Frieze New York comes back in May, at The Shed, with a preview on Wednesday and public days that follow, that's where megagalleries and emerging ones cross paths most, inside the same building. And in December, the big date is Art Basel Miami Beach, with VIP days on December second and third and public days from the fourth through the sixth, the week where a big chunk of the year's work with international clients gets packed in. There again, thirty four billion, eight hundred million dollars is the number to keep in your head, because it explains why so many major collectors build their calendar around these fairs, not just around the November sale.

How does someone who's good at this job prepare. Marc Glimcher, the president of Pace, said they raise a young artist's prices carefully, around ten percent a year, so they don't burn the artist out once the initial excitement cools off. And an advisor with fair experience, Esthella Provas, explained that before she sets foot in the hall she's already read the PDFs the galleries sent, taken notes, and worked out a priority order with the client, the big galleries first, where the good work sells fast, then open time to discover something new.

This is useful with a client when they ask whether it's better to wait for the November sale or buy now at a fair. That's where you explain they're two different games, at the fair the gallery sets the price and you get in through relationship, at the auction the price is set by public competition. You might say something like, "if this piece really matters to you, let's see it at the Armory next month, before the auction sets a price you might not want to pay".

And there's another question they'll ask you for sure, why go with you instead of alone. That's where a good advisor earns her keep, it's not about tagging along, it's reading the material beforehand, filtering out what's worth seeing from the four hundred plus galleries a major fair puts together, and taking the client straight to what serves them. That's time a collector with a real job doesn't have, and it's exactly what Phillips expects you to know how to offer.
