Week three, lesson one.

Yesterday's recap. You told your client to look first at Amaral and Fordjour with confidence, and to just keep an eye on Gego without rushing to pay as if the price were already about to move... why the difference, if all three carry the same museum weight? Because Amaral and Fordjour already repeated their prices across more than one house and more than one year, while Gego's record has stood since twenty seventeen without repeating. A museum certifies interest, it does not predict price, and that distinction is what separated advice built on data from a hunch with a nice name attached.

Before she leaves the reception, the same client stops you with a new question. At the same fair she saw two versions of an already established Latin American artist, one of the names that keep showing up on your radar these past weeks. One is a large oil. The other is a portfolio of numbered prints of the same image. She asks you which one you would recommend to a client of hers with a lot less to spend. You know they are not the same investment, but you have never had to explain clearly why.

A large oil is an object that exists once in the entire world. If that client buys it, there is no other one anywhere, and that uniqueness alone carries much of the price. A portfolio of numbered prints is a different thing. The artist, or someone working under the artist's direction, printed the same image several times from the same plate or stone, signed it, and numbered it, and that full edition can run to twenty, fifty, or a hundred identical copies out in the world. The more copies exist, the less scarce each one is, and the price drops accordingly, even when the signature and the image are exactly the same as the oil's. That is the hierarchy that splits a single established artist into several price tiers by medium, the oil at the very top, a unique work on paper, a hand-made drawing or watercolor that was never repeated, on a middle step, and editions at the bottom, with the exception of short, already sold-out runs by a highly sought-after artist, which sometimes climb higher than expected.

But inside that same edition, not every copy is worth the same either. That brings in another kind of look, the physical condition of that specific impression, whether it still has its original margins, whether the paper has yellowed over time, whether someone trimmed it down to fit a frame, whether it went through a restoration. The document that describes all of that is called a condition report, and before recommending any piece to a client, in any medium, you need to request it and read it in full. It does not certify authenticity or guarantee the work is free of a problem the naked eye missed, but it tells you what you are dealing with before price ever comes up.

Look at what happened this past June with Phillips' Editions and Works on Paper sale in New York. It totaled one million four hundred forty four thousand and twenty six dollars, placed eighty nine percent of the lots and ninety seven percent of the value on offer, and that same afternoon it set twenty new artist records. In that same sale, a print by William Kentridge, the South African artist known for his animations made from charcoal drawings erased and rephotographed, was estimated at one to two thousand dollars and sold for one thousand ninety six. An editioned poster by Marcel Duchamp, the French artist whose retrospective closes at MoMA today, was estimated at five hundred to seven hundred dollars and sold for three hundred eighty seven, below the low estimate. None of those prices comes anywhere close to what an oil by those same artists is worth, and the sale still performed well overall, with nearly everything placed and new records falling one after another. Phillips reports that forty one percent of the people who bought editions in twenty twenty five were new clients for the house. That is the entry door, the medium through which someone starts buying before working up to an oil, and that is why it pays for the house to keep that category active even though each individual piece is worth little next to an evening sale.

That is where the limit you need to draw whenever you talk about editions comes in. The fact that the Duchamp copy sold below estimate does not mean the artist lost value, or that the entire editions market cooled off that afternoon. It means that specific impression, in its specific condition, found that specific price on that specific day. What you can say with today's numbers is that the category as a whole, taken together, is still selling well and still setting records.

Back to your client and her client with the smaller budget. First you ask how much she wants to spend and for what, decoration or the start of a real collection. If the budget will not stretch to the oil, the portfolio of prints is not a consolation prize, it is a legitimate way into that same artist, as long as her client understands she is buying one of several identical copies and not a one-of-a-kind piece. You explain the difference in medium in exactly those terms, no jargon, something like this, an oil is a single piece in the entire world, while this portfolio is one of several identical copies, so the price will never play in the same league, even though the signature is the very same signature. And you tell her that before closing either deal, she should request the condition report for that specific piece, not a generic sheet on the artist. What you cannot promise her is that the portfolio of prints will rise in price the way the oil would, because it does not compete in the same category or carry the same scarcity.

Your client thanks you and, before saying goodbye, mentions that a dealer separately offered her an artist's proof of that same print, outside the official numbered edition, and asked whether that is worth more or less than a regular numbered copy.
