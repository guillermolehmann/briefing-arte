Week four, exam day. Ten mixed questions on what you covered this week. Answer out loud before you hear the answer.

Yesterday you built your own pitch as an advisor, applying to yourself the same structure you used all week to present a work. What are the three fixed parts of that pitch, and in what order do they go?............ History, comparables, and why now, always in that order. Starting with price puts the client on the defensive, while starting with history hooks them before you show the evidence behind the number, and that's why the order never flips, whether you're presenting a work or presenting yourself.

On Monday you built the framework of the four collector types, passionate, decorator, investor, and social. What did Eduardo Costantini announce this past December, and what effect did it have on MALBA?............ The acquisition of the Daros Latinamerica collection, based in Zurich, one thousand two hundred thirty three works by one hundred seventeen artists, which doubled in one stroke the holdings of the museum he himself founded in Buenos Aires. It's the textbook case of the passionate collector taken to its fullest expression, a private collection that ends up generating and sustaining a public institution meant to last.

On Tuesday you worked on first contact with a new client, discovery versus interrogation. According to Carola Wiese, senior art specialist at UBS Global Wealth Management, what characterizes a good advisor with someone they've just met?............ Studying, listening, and trying to stay objective, letting the client's own story reveal their budget and taste indirectly, instead of leading with a battery of direct questions or the advisor's own aesthetic taste from the start of the conversation.

On Wednesday you built the full pitch for Adriana Varejão for a client from the Armory Show. What recent comparable did you use instead of her twenty eleven record, and why did you prefer that figure?............ A piece sold at Phillips London on June the eighth of this year for three hundred seventy four thousand one hundred dollars. A twenty eleven record describes an exceptional sale from more than a decade ago, under conditions that no longer exist, while a result from this year describes what a real buyer is willing to pay today for a comparable piece, which is exactly what makes a price conversation credible.

Back to Tuesday for a moment. David Schrader, Sotheby's head of private sales, turns a vague preference into a concrete search. Into which six data points does he convert that request, and what does he activate with them afterward?............ Artist, series, year, size, color, and budget, and with those six coordinates he activates a global network of relationship managers spread across different offices around the world, to locate that exact piece or that exact buyer, with discretion tailored to each specific request.

On Thursday you worked through the four most common objections in a sales conversation. What are they, and what do they have in common beyond sounding different?............ That the work is expensive, that it might be fake, that it might be worth less later, and that the client needs to check with a third party. Beyond sounding different, all four ask for the same kind of thing, an implicit request for proof, and the professional answer delivers that proof instead of a verbal reassurance based on your own opinion.

Yesterday, in your own pitch, how many times more did Sotheby's and Christie's bill than Phillips according to the twenty twenty five year end results, and which records does your own house still hold despite that gap?............ Sotheby's bills about seven and a half times what Phillips does, and Christie's about six and a half times, but Phillips still holds the auction records for the sculptor Lygia Clark and the sculptor Doris Salcedo. A house's total scale doesn't decide who holds a given Latin American artist's record, that depends on whether that house happened to be the meeting point between that specific work and the buyer willing to pay that price.

From Thursday, on the joint Bank of America and ArtTactic study, what was the difference in average annual return between works held twenty to twenty five years and those sold before five years?............ Eleven percent average annual return against negative five point seven percent for the short holding period group. It's an average across an entire market, not a guarantee for any one specific work, and that distinction always has to be made clear before using the figure in front of a client.

A client tells you, mid conversation, that the work you showed her feels expensive. With everything you covered this week on objections and on the pitch, how do you answer her?............ You back the figure with at least two recent, dated comparables, never with your own opinion as the seller, citing the two results from this year that fall in the same price band instead of a historical record. And if the doubt shifts toward authenticity or future value, each one gets its own evidence, provenance documentation for the first and the return by holding period data for the second, always making clear that neither one guarantees a specific outcome for that particular work.

And the last one, on discovery. A new client comes to you referred by someone else, with no prior information about their taste or their budget. What do you avoid doing on that first call, and what do you do instead?............ You avoid the battery of direct, back to back questions that can fill out a form but leave the client feeling interrogated. Instead you let their own story about their collecting history reveal budget and taste indirectly, and only afterward, if more precision is needed, do you turn that listening into a concrete brief of artist, series, year, size, color, and budget, never as the first step of the conversation.
