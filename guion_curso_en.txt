Week one, review. Selling Art in New York.

Picture your phone ringing and it's a collector from Bogotá you're meeting in person for the first time at the Armory Show, in late September. She tells you one thing, that she wants to understand how all of this works before she spends a dollar. You have a month to get ready, and today we're pulling together this week's five lessons so that when she asks, the answer comes out whole, without hesitation.

First, the full map. Remember how a work of art circulates and where you stand in it? The circuit has five stations, galleries, which make up the primary market when they sell something for the first time, auction houses, which make up the secondary market when that work resells, fairs, which compress the primary market into a single week, museums, which don't sell but validate, and advisors, who guide the buyer through every step. You work at Phillips, on the auction side, in the secondary market, alongside Christie's and Sotheby's. That map is the first thing you show your collector, so she understands which part of the circuit each conversation she has at the fair belongs to.

Second, the three houses and why scale isn't everything. Christie's and Sotheby's each move several times what Phillips moves, seven or eight times each, nearly fourteen times combined. But Phillips doesn't compete on volume, it competes on selection, and back in May it proved it with an evening sale that sold one hundred percent of its lots, what the trade calls a white glove sale. That strategy is backed up by Editions, the department where Phillips sells work starting in the low thousands, which last year brought in forty one percent buyers who were completely new to the house, an entry point neither Christie's nor Sotheby's documents as clearly. This matters for your collector because it explains why you work where you work, with a strategy different from the two giants, more selective, built around artists whose market is still forming.

Third, the auction from the inside. Here's the number people forget most, the price the auctioneer announces isn't what the buyer actually pays. The buyer's premium stacks on top of the hammer in tiers, and a five hundred thousand dollar hammer at Phillips ends up costing six hundred forty five thousand before taxes. There's also the reserve, the confidential floor the consignor negotiates, distinct from the estimate published in the catalog, and the difference between an evening sale, the curated, ceremonial part of the week, and a day sale, with more lots and less ceremony but just as solid. If your collector is bidding at Armory on a gallery work there's no hammer involved, but if she ever decides to sell something at auction, this is the number she needs straight before she signs anything.

Fourth, galleries and fairs, which is exactly where you'll be walking beside her a month from now. The primary and secondary markets coexist, and in fact dealers and galleries generate more money combined than auction houses do, thirty four billion eight hundred million dollars against well under twenty five billion between the auction houses' public and private sales. That's useful for taking the shine off the saleroom with a client who only pays attention to auctions. And for the fair itself, the key is preparation ahead of time, looking early at what each gallery is bringing and building a priority order with your collector, because the most sought after pieces tend to sell in the first days.

And fifth, from last week, remember the Olga de Amaral case? Her retrospective at the Fondation Cartier in Paris preceded a new auction record by about a year, three million one hundred twenty five thousand dollars for her piece Pueblo H. With Kahlo it went the other way, the record came first and the MoMA show came after. And with Julio Le Parc the institutional signal, his Tate Modern retrospective, still hasn't found a match in price. That last case is the one most worth repeating to an anxious client, a museum honor doesn't guarantee anything overnight. And so you're not left depending on news reaching you by chance, three fixed places to check every week, Artnet News for auction results, The Art Newspaper for the institutional side, and ArtTactic for the deeper market reports.

Now put the five pieces together in your collector's scene at Armory. She walks into the fair and spots a work she likes at a gallery she's never heard of. First you place where that sale sits in the circuit, primary market, priced by the gallery, not by public bidding. Then you explain that if she decides to resell it later, that's when Christie's, Sotheby's and Phillips come into play, with their rules on estimate, reserve and buyer's premium. You tell her that if the gallery is small, it probably doesn't have a megagallery's scale, and that size simply reflects a different business model, the same argument you use to explain why Phillips doesn't compete dollar for dollar with the two giants. And if the artist had a recent museum retrospective, you flag it as a point in the work's favor, without promising a price jump that hasn't happened yet. You might tell her something like this, this gallery sells at a fixed price with no bidding, so what matters today is the relationship with them, but if in a few years you decide to resell it, a different game kicks in, with estimate, reserve and tiered commission, and that game I'll walk you through.
