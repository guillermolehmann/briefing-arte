Before we start, two questions. Yesterday you saw Jackson Pollock's record next to Basquiat's stalled ceiling. What separates an isolated record from a sustained trend?... That it repeats across more than one artist from the same movement, not just one, the way it happened with Pollock and Rothko the same week while Basquiat stayed stuck at the same ceiling since two thousand seventeen. And from a few days back, why does Sotheby's build an entire major consignment around Picasso and Matisse?... Because their names guarantee buyer attention and anchor the price for the whole sale, even when the rest of the catalogue is smaller.

A client writes to you today. She saw on Instagram that Phillips sold another work by a painter who's still alive and active, at a higher price than last time, and she asks if that means it's already the moment to buy something of his before it climbs further.

To answer her well you need to separate two things that look alike but aren't the same. An auction house can sell a living artist's work once, at a high price, and that alone tells you nothing yet about the rest of his market. But when that same house places the same artist again, in an important sale, and the price that second time beats the first, and then it happens a third time, something different starts forming, a track record of its own. That's what separates a work that got lucky one night from an artist whose market is being built for real. This can be called an artist's revaluation, and with living artists it's harder to read than with a master who's already passed, because there aren't thirty years of auction results to look back on yet, so every new sale carries more weight than it would in an already settled market.

The clearest case of this right now is Derek Fordjour, an American painter who works with collage and burlap textures over sports figures and crowd scenes. In May two thousand twenty four, Phillips sold his work "Numbers" for eight hundred and eighty nine thousand dollars. Six months later, in November of that same year, they placed another of his works, "Twelve Tribes," and this time the price rose to one million one hundred and forty three thousand dollars, twenty percent above the artist's previous record, which dated to two thousand twenty two and belonged to Christie's. Two sales, two different houses, the second always beating the first. That's a track record, not a one night coincidence.

Now the contrast, so you don't walk away thinking any second sale is enough. Firelei Báez is a Dominican painter based in New York, known for portraits that blend colonial Caribbean cartography with plant motifs and textile patterns. In November two thousand twenty five, Phillips sold her work "Daughter of Revolutions" for six hundred and forty five thousand dollars, beating her previous record. Up to that point, the same pattern as Fordjour. But that same day, in that same November season, Christie's placed another of her works, "Untitled, Colonization in America," for one million one hundred and ten thousand dollars, far above what Phillips had achieved just hours earlier. The actual high number of the day landed at a different house.

Separate the three things before you draw a conclusion. What you know for certain is that both artists had a recent sale that beat their previous record. What's reasonable to think is that Fordjour has already built a firmer base, because two different houses confirmed the same direction in different years. What you still can't prove is where Báez is headed, because one strong afternoon, however strong, isn't enough to call it a trend, and that Christie's jump the same day could just as easily reflect her market taking off as it could reflect that particular work having something special another one won't repeat. With Fordjour, two houses and two years are already confirming the same direction. With Báez, for now, there's a single high snapshot of one afternoon.

This connects to something you're going to hear more and more around the trade, the idea of "wet paint." That's the term for a work that gets resold at auction very soon after leaving the artist's studio, sometimes with as little as a year or two between the painting and the sale. That one resale goes well once doesn't prove the artist has a solid market, because there hasn't been time yet for several buyers, across several houses, to confirm the same number. Neither Fordjour nor Báez is a wet paint case, both have been painting for more than a decade and already carry several auction seasons behind them, but the logic is the same as with an artist fresh out of grad school, the more times a price repeats, across more different places, the less it looks like coincidence.

So if that client asks whether it's worth buying now from the painter she saw on Instagram, don't say yes or no right away. Ask her first which one she means, because the answer changes with the track record, and for that you can use the same question you learned with the layered circuit back in week one, where in that circuit does this work stand and who else backs it besides a single house. If it's someone with two or three results holding up across more than one house over more than a year, like Fordjour, you can tell her something like this, "two different houses are confirming the same price in two different years, that's already a real base." If it's a name with just one strong, recent sale, the answer changes, "it's still one afternoon, let's wait for the next sale at another house before treating that level as a confirmed floor."
