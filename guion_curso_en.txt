Week two, lesson four.

Yesterday left an open situation. A client offers you, through a waitlist, the chance to buy a work by a thirty year old painter with the promise that in two years it will triple in value... You tell her that no auction history backs that promise yet, because a thirty year career is just getting started, and until that artist repeats strong results across more than one house and more than one year, talking about tripling is a bet, not a fact.

A Colombian client writes you, excited. She saw the news that London is opening the first Fernando Botero show in that city since two thousand fifteen this October, and she asks whether that means she should sell the Botero she inherited from her grandfather now, before the price climbs any higher.

To answer her well you need to understand what separates an established artist from a passing trend, and that starts well before you put a name on the idea. Picture two artists with an auction record. The first notches seven figure sales every year, across more than one house, and on top of that two different galleries are mounting shows of his work in the same season. The second had one spectacular sale a decade ago and barely surfaced at auction since. A buyer who looks only at the record sees two equally strong names, but a buyer who looks at what happened afterward sees something very different, because only the first one guarantees that if you want to resell down the road, you'll find real interest and buyers willing to pay again. That's called market depth, and it's what separates a truly established name from one that had a good night.

Fernando Botero is the clearest example of depth right now. His record was set by "The Musicians," one of his signature inflated figures playing instruments, sold at Christie's in two thousand twenty three for five million one hundred and thirty two thousand dollars. What matters is what happened after. Between two thousand twenty four and two thousand twenty five, Christie's sold five more of his paintings, ranging from six hundred and ninety three thousand to one million seven hundred and forty four thousand dollars, none close to the record but all in seven figures. That repetition, not the record alone, is the proof that demand is constant. And this week a third signal arrived, institutional instead of commercial. Opera Gallery in London announced that Botero opens there in October, with paintings, drawings and sculptures spanning nearly six decades of his career, his first show in that city in eleven years, at the same time the Sotheby's show here in New York is still running. When an artist has repeated sales and two different institutions showing his work the same season, that reads as an institutional signal reinforcing depth, not a calendar coincidence.

To keep the region's other established names in your head, three more records, each from a single exceptional work. Diego Rivera, the Mexican muralist known for his frescoes of factories and peasants on public buildings across Mexico and the United States, fourteen million one hundred and thirty thousand dollars for a painting at Christie's in two thousand twenty two. Rufino Tamayo, also Mexican, seven million two hundred and nine thousand dollars, a record dating back to two thousand eight that nobody has beaten since. Wifredo Lam, the Cuban who crossed surrealism with African and Caribbean references, nine million six hundred and three thousand eight hundred dollars since two thousand twenty. None of the three carries, in recent years, the chain of repeated sales that Botero has now, so saying their names isn't enough to talk about an active market today, it's enough to talk about history and institutional weight.

That's where the contrast worth flagging comes in. Roberto Matta, the Chilean surrealist, has held his own record since two thousand twelve, five million ten thousand five hundred dollars. But this past May a painting of his, "Convict the Impossible," sold at Sotheby's for one hundred and forty thousand eight hundred dollars, well below the two hundred and forty one thousand a buyer had paid for that same work back in two thousand seven. The name is still established, the signature is still recognizable, and that particular painting still dropped more than forty percent against its own previous sale. Depth exists at the level of the artist, but it doesn't protect every individual work from losing value.

What you can't conclude from any of this is that Rivera's, Tamayo's or Lam's old record is about to repeat itself soon, or that the London show will lift the price of every Botero painting equally, or that Matta is in general decline just because one work sold lower than before. Every painting gets judged by its period, its size, its provenance and its condition, not by the artist's last name or by which city the next show opens in. An institutional signal helps you read the general climate, but it never replaces looking at the specific work in front of you.

So you can tell your client, with confidence, that Botero holds a record above five million dollars and repeated seven figure sales every year, and that this season adds two institutional shows, one in New York and one in London. What you still need to verify is authenticity with the Botero Foundation, which manages the artist's estate, the exact period of the inherited work within the nearly seven decades he painted, and whether it's an oil, a drawing or a sculpture, because each medium carries its own price range. And where you need to be careful is not promising her the price will rise just because there's a new show, because that hasn't happened yet and it might not. What you can tell her instead is that now is a good moment to get it appraised and start a conversation with the Foundation, with the London show as a concrete reason to open it.

And if that conversation ever turns into an actual consignment, that's where Phillips comes in. The house doesn't compete on volume with Christie's or Sotheby's in this segment, but a well documented Botero, with clear provenance, coming out of a family collection, is exactly the kind of piece that can build a compelling catalogue story, regardless of which house it ultimately sells through.
