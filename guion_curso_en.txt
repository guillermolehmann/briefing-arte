Week three, exam day. Ten mixed questions on everything you covered this week. Answer out loud before you hear the answer.

Yesterday's private sale client already has that same work pledged as collateral on a bank loan. Can he still consign it for the private sale, or does the bank get a say first?............ The bank gets a say first. A work pledged as collateral carries a lien in favor of whoever lent the money, and no sale, public or private, can close without the bank's consent or without paying off the debt with part of the proceeds. Neither the discretion of a private sale nor the speed of any channel gets around that step, because the art-backed lending you covered yesterday comes with this same fine print attached.

What did Phillips' Editions and Works on Paper sale of June the twenty fourth total, and what percentage of the lots found buyers?............ One million four hundred forty four thousand twenty six dollars, with eighty nine percent of the lots placed and ninety seven percent of the value sold, and that same session set twenty new artist records. None of those lots comes anywhere near what an oil by an artist of that stature would fetch, and the session still ran with completely normal commercial results.

On the commission a house charges the seller when a work is consigned, which of the three majors is the only one that publishes a fixed rate, and how much is it?............ Sotheby's, at ten percent of the hammer price, with a five hundred dollar minimum on smaller lots and an additional two percent success fee if the result beats the agreed high estimate. Christie's and Phillips negotiate it case by case depending on the value and category of the work, so in front of a client that figure should never be presented as a fixed number unless the consignment is going to Sotheby's.

What happened in April of two thousand twenty four at the SP-Arte fair in São Paulo with a painting attributed to Tarsila do Amaral?............ The founder of a rival auction house publicly questioned its authenticity without having examined it up close, while the gallery was offering it privately to specific buyers for around three point two million dollars, away from the fair's official walkthrough. Neither the seller's word nor the doubter's was enough to settle anything on its own, and the episode shows that the higher an artist's benchmark price climbs, the greater the incentive both to pass off a doubtful piece as genuine and to publicly question a legitimate one for one's own commercial gain.

How did David Schrader, Sotheby's head of private sales, describe the method for tracking down a specific work?............ As a sourcing job done to order. First the team turns the client's interest into a precise brief, artist, series, year, size, and color, and only then activates an internal network of relationship managers spread across different offices around the world to locate that exact piece or that exact buyer, with discretion tailored to each request.

On New York sales tax, what does the destination rule say, and how much tax applies to a hundred thousand dollar purchase delivered in Manhattan?............ What decides the tax is the place where the house actually delivers the work, not the buyer's home address or citizenship. In Manhattan the combined rate is eight point eight seven five percent, split between the state, the city, and a small metropolitan transit share, so on a hundred thousand dollars delivered right there, that comes to eight thousand eight hundred seventy five dollars in tax, on top of the purchase price.

Within the work of a single established artist, why is a numbered edition worth less than an oil even with the same signature and the same image?............ Because a unique oil or sculpture has no equivalent anywhere else in the world, and that absolute scarcity underpins the highest part of the artist's price curve, while an edition is a multiple impression from the same plate or matrix, and the more copies exist of the same image, the lower the scarcity of each one, even when the signature, the date, and the image are identical to those of a unique piece.

Which two lots went up marked at once as Guaranteed Property and with Irrevocable Bids at Sotheby's in May of two thousand twenty six?............ Henri Matisse's La Séance du matin, in the Modern Evening Auction, and Andy Warhol's Flowers, Six Works, in the Contemporary Day Auction, showing that the combination of both symbols, a guaranteed price floor plus a purchase commitment signed ahead of the sale, isn't limited to the nine figure lots of an evening sale, it also shows up in day sales at a much more accessible price point.

A client with a sculpture that only interests a handful of collectors worldwide asks you whether it makes more sense to auction it publicly or look for a private buyer. What do you tell her?............ That a public auction performs best when there's a wide universe of plausible buyers competing against each other, and that with only a handful of possible buyers worldwide it's worth reconsidering that route, following the criterion advisor Patti Wong proposes. And that either way, whichever channel gets chosen, a private sale still doesn't replace a serious appraisal of the work done by an independent professional.

A client offers to quickly close on a painting by an established Latin American master, without a complete provenance chain, at a very attractive price. What do you recommend before discussing price at all?............ First check whether the work appears in the artist's catalogue raisonné or whether there's a ruling from whoever administers the estate, request documentation for every change of ownership with no unexplained gaps of years, and given any meaningful doubt given the amount at stake, suggest an independent technical review before moving the price upward. It's the same kind of episode that triggered the public dispute in two thousand twenty four over the painting attributed to Tarsila do Amaral.
