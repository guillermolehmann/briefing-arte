Week two, lesson two. Selling Art in New York.

Before we start, a question about yesterday's entry. Yesterday we talked about Sotheby's landing the Blaquier collection, four hundred and fifty million dollars across Van Gogh, Cézanne, Degas, Renoir and Pissarro... why does a collection like that end up at Sotheby's and not at Phillips? Because Sotheby's and Christie's run at a scale Phillips does not chase, with teams and buyers built to move consignments of that size within the Impressionist and Modern category, while Phillips built its place in twentieth and twenty first century art, a smaller but more selective market.

A young client writes to you thrilled, sends you a headline about a Pollock that sold for a staggering sum, and asks whether he should buy a Rothko or a Basquiat, since they are all from the same period and all he cares about is that the price keeps climbing. You answer with what you are about to learn today.

After the Second World War, the center of the art world moved from Paris to New York. Museums like MoMA organized touring shows that carried the work of a group of New York painters across Europe, and that sustained exposure, show after show, decade after decade, built a consensus, first among curators and then among buyers, that those paintings deserved the same standing as the European names you have already been studying. That is institutional consecration, when a body of work spends years accumulating museum shows, catalogues and academic study before the market prices it at a level that matches that reputation. Only after all of that did anyone put a name to the movement, Abstract Expressionism, with Jackson Pollock and his drips, Mark Rothko and his blocks of color, and Willem de Kooning and his violent brushwork as its three best known faces. A few years later a different phenomenon appeared with Andy Warhol, who painted not scenes or gestures but images everyone already knew, a soup can, a bottle, Marilyn Monroe's face repeated in series. There, price no longer depends only on museum approval, it depends on millions of people recognizing that image even if they have never set foot in a gallery, and that widens the pool of buyers willing to compete for it far beyond the traditional circle of art collectors.

The same week as that Pollock, another pillar of Abstract Expressionism confirmed the same pattern. A Mark Rothko canvas, one of those paintings with two or three blocks of color floating one over another, also sold at Christie's and broke the artist's previous record, which had stood for fourteen years, pushing it up thirteen percent. Two records broken the same week, at the same house, within the same category, is a sign that the capital available to buy the top of Abstract Expressionism keeps growing.

The case that best shows how far that consecration reaches is the Pollock your client sent you. This past May, Christie's auctioned a painting of his from nineteen forty eight, a large oil more than three meters wide, for one hundred and eighty one million two hundred thousand dollars, nearly triple the artist's previous record, which dated to twenty twenty one and stood around sixty one million. The work came from a New York collector's estate and went to sale with a one hundred million dollar estimate, and even so several bidders kept pushing well past that number. A result like that needs both things at once, a scarce and exceptional painting, and decades of institutional reputation that give any buyer confidence they are paying for something with proven history.

Now watch out for the easy conclusion, because fame and institutional consecration do not always move price the same way. Basquiat is the perfect counterexample. His record of one hundred and ten and a half million dollars, set in twenty seventeen, still stands today, nine years later, even though he remains one of the most sought after and most cited names in any conversation about contemporary art. This past May, another painting of his sold for fifty two million seven hundred thousand dollars, a high figure that confirms the market stays strong beneath that ceiling, but the ceiling itself has not moved once in nearly a decade. Fame and cultural presence sustain a market, they do not guarantee that a work capable of beating the previous record will always turn up.

So let's separate what we know from what is reasonable to think and from what cannot be claimed. We know that particular Pollock sold for one hundred and eighty one million, with a truly rare painting behind it. It is reasonable to think that the combination of scarcity and institutional reputation explains a large part of that figure. What cannot be claimed is that every Abstract Expressionist painting is now worth much more just because one of them broke a record, nor that Basquiat's market is stuck or falling just because his ceiling has not moved in nine years, because May's sale showed strong demand well below that ceiling.

With your client, this turns into not letting the excitement of a headline lead him to treat an entire movement as if it were a single asset. You can put it this way, I understand the excitement, but before we talk about a Rothko or a Basquiat I need to see the actual history of the specific work you have in mind, because someone else's record does not lend its price to just any piece by the same artist or the same period. And if he pushes back saying he wants to buy planning to resell soon, you have a useful figure to ease that anxiety, in the holding period studies we already covered, works that stayed in the same collection for twenty to twenty five years returned eleven percent a year on average, while ones sold before the five year mark lost close to six percent on average. Art rewards patience far more than it rewards a quick exit.

That same client is going to call you again next week with a different question, because he found a twenty five year old artist just starting to show at a fair and wants to buy something planning to resell it within two years. Think through what you would tell him using what you learned today about patience and about what a record actually proves.
