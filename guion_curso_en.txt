The painting that sold nine months ago for fifty-four million six hundred and sixty thousand dollars now hangs for free, for anyone who buys a fourteen-dollar ticket, in a gallery at MoMA.

Before we go on, yesterday's question... do you remember the difference between the primary and secondary market, and what the gallery Perrotin did with its Second Marché unit? The primary market is a gallery's direct sale to a buyer of a work coming to market for the first time. The secondary market is the resale of something that already changed hands at least once. Perrotin built a separate division, Second Marché, dedicated only to resale, after noticing that even its biggest collectors were getting split attention, one person sold them new work and someone completely different handled buying back or reselling the old.

And since we're on lesson five, here's a second question, from further back... do you remember why Christie's and Sotheby's bring in so much more revenue than Phillips, and why that's not necessarily a problem? Each of the two big houses moves seven to eight times what Phillips moves, and combined, almost fourteen times more. But Phillips plays the selection game, not the volume game, betting hard on young artists and on Editions instead of competing number against number with the two giants.

Today's idea is just one. A retrospective at a major museum isn't just prestige, it's a signal that moves price, and you need to know how to read it before your client asks you about it.

First movement, Bogotá connects directly with New York. Olga de Amaral has worked with fiber and gold leaf since the nineteen-sixties, mostly outside the radar of major auctions. In two thousand twenty-four, the Fondation Cartier in Paris gave her a major retrospective, the first of that scale outside Colombia, which kept traveling through European museums during two thousand twenty-four and two thousand twenty-five. About a year later, on November nineteenth, two thousand twenty-five, Christie's sold her piece Pueblo H in New York for three million one hundred and twenty-five thousand dollars, a new record for the artist and more than double what she was worth before. It wasn't a coincidence, it was the retrospective turning into price.

So far, then, a well-timed retrospective can push a price upward, and Amaral's case proves it with numbers.

Second movement, two cases that show the same mechanism from different angles. With Kahlo it happened backwards from Amaral, the record came first, and the museum's recognition came after. El sueño, la cama sold at Sotheby's in New York on November twentieth, two thousand twenty-five, for fifty-four million six hundred and sixty thousand dollars. Four months later, MoMA opened Frida and Diego, The Last Dream, a show bringing together several Kahlo pieces, running through September twelfth of this year. The record enabled the show, and now the show sustains that record for whoever the next buyer turns out to be. Julio Le Parc, on the other hand, shows the case still unresolved. The Argentine, a pioneer of kinetic art, has a major retrospective scheduled at the Tate Modern in London starting June two thousand twenty-six, the strongest institutional signal of his entire career, but his auction record is still stuck at five hundred and six thousand five hundred dollars, from two thousand ten. There, the retrospective arrived before the market did, and that's also something you need to know how to read with a client, without promising something that hasn't happened yet. It can take months, it can take years, and sometimes it doesn't happen at all, so it's better to treat it as a signal worth following closely, not as a promise already made.

And so you don't depend on the news reaching you by chance, there are three fixed places to check every week. Artnet News covers auction results almost in real time, and also runs its own index, the Fine Art Top 100, which over the last twenty years returned just three point two percent annualized, well below the ten point four percent of the S&P 500 over the same period, a good figure for when a client talks to you about art as an investment. The Art Newspaper is the reference for institutional coverage, museums, acquisitions, new shows. And ArtTactic produces the deep reports, the same ones we've been citing throughout this course, on wealth management and art finance.

This serves you with a client when they ask you why suddenly everyone's talking about an artist they'd had hanging on their wall for years without paying much attention. That's when you explain that there's almost always a museum retrospective behind it, and that it's not just prestige, it's a signal the market reads and that ends up pushing the price. You could say something like, "notice how your Amaral hung at the Fondation Cartier a couple of years ago, and last year a piece of hers sold for triple what it used to go for, your piece is worth something different now."
