Week four, lesson two.

Yesterday's question. The client who asked whether the painting matched his living room decor called back the next day with a different question... what was it?... whether that same piece had room to rise in price over the next few years. And yesterday's class left you wondering whether you still saw him as purely a decorator, or whether that changed the conversation. The right answer isn't picking a new label, because almost nobody is only one of the four types. It's listening for which motivation weighs heaviest in this particular question, without throwing out the earlier one, and taking the conversation from there.

Picture your phone ringing on a Tuesday morning. On the other end, a collector from Bogotá you've never spoken to, whom Marisol, your client with the inherited Soto, simply told to call you. You know nothing about her, not how much she collects, not what she's looking for, not what she can spend. You have two paths. One is firing off a list of questions back to back, how much do you spend, which artists do you follow, are you buying for return, and hanging up with a full file and a client who felt interrogated. The other is letting her tell her own story and picking up, along the way, the information you need without it ever feeling like you're digging for it.

What changes between those two calls is the order the questions arrive in, not how many there are. When you let her talk first, about her connection to Marisol, about why that particular Soto caught her eye, about what's already on her own walls, the signals you need start showing up on their own. The real budget shows up behind a phrase like "something significant, but not crazy," and the taste shows up behind "I like work that does a lot with very little." You take it in quietly and only come back with a pointed question when you need to pin down a fact, never as an opener. That way of listening first and asking later is known in the trade as discovery, and it works better than an interrogation because the person feels like you're paying attention to her, not filling out a form. There's an added trap that beginners fall into, dropping in their own opinion too soon, telling the Bogotá collector that you love a certain artist or that another one is overpriced before you even know what moves her. What you end up recommending has to come from her own language and her own story, not from taste you already had sitting in your back pocket.

Evan Tawil, an independent art advisor and private dealer in New York, built much of his client roster leaning on that same kind of listening. As he told Artnet News in an interview published on October twentieth, two thousand twenty three, his first advisory clients came through his own network, and he still picks up new clients today through referrals from earlier ones. The reason he gives is simple, he responds fast, and from the first contact he's clear about what he can actually deliver and what he can't. When a request isn't workable, he says so early instead of drawing out the conversation with vague talk, something he says happens with a good share of the requests he gets, and that early honesty saves both sides time instead of getting someone's hopes up only to disappoint them later. Tawil also avoids reopening a deal once terms are set by pushing for better conditions at the last minute, because he knows a single late renegotiation can shut the door on the next referral. Responding fast, being honest about what you can and can't do, and following through on what you agreed to without going back on it, is what turns a first call like the one from the Bogotá collector into a relationship that keeps bringing more calls.

There's a second way to use that same kind of listening, described by David Schrader, head of private sales at Sotheby's, the auction house. When a client tells him she's after something by a particular artist without quite knowing what, he turns that into what he calls a sourcing brief, with the artist, series, year, size, color, and budget all defined, and only then does he activate a global network of contacts to track down the specific piece, in private deals where neither the buyer's name nor the work's ever becomes public. The difference with Tawil is one of timescale. Schrader turns a vague preference into a concrete specification within a single conversation, while Tawil builds his reputation over years of responding the same way, call after call, much like today's.

What you can't take away from these two cases is that asking a lot of questions guarantees the sale, or that one well-handled call automatically brings you a referral. Tawil's reputation was built over years of responding that way, not from a single call. And Schrader's brief carries its own risk, if you narrow it too much, this artist, this size, this year, you can end up ruling out a different work that same collector would have happily accepted if you'd shown it to her. Listening well doesn't replace the judgment that comes after.

Back to the Bogotá collector. You ask her, unhurried, how she came to be interested in Marisol's Soto, and she tells you she has two small kinetic pieces at home and has been looking for something that speaks to those two without repeating them. Right there you already have half a brief without having asked directly about budget, artist, or size. You can tell her with confidence that you'll look for options that speak to what she already has at home. What you still need to find out is the real budget, and you ask it only at that point, naturally, framed as a range, something like "so I don't waste your time showing you things that are off the mark, are we talking about something in the range of Marisol's Soto, or a different scale." And where it pays to be careful is not promising her a specific piece until you've confirmed it exists and is available, because saying "let me see what I can find and I'll get back to you in a few days" protects the relationship better than getting her hopes up over something that might not come together.
