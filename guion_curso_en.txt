Week three, lesson two.

Quick recap. Yesterday you saw the difference between a unique oil and an edition by the same artist, why can a numbered print still be expensive even though it is worth less than the full painting?... Because the scarcity is different, an oil is a single object and an edition is one of several identical copies pulled from the same plate, but the signature, the number, and the condition of that specific copy still set its own price within the group. And we said one more thing, does a condition report matter for an oil, for an edition, or for both?... For both, because the physical condition of that specific piece weighs on the price no matter the medium.

Yesterday left a question hanging. That same dealer offered your client an artist's proof of the same print, a copy marked separately, outside the normal numbering of the edition, and told her that is why it is worth more. She calls you before answering him. She does not know if that is true, whether that artist's proof genuinely exists as such, or whether the dealer simply wrote that label on some random copy to charge more. Before talking price, there is an earlier question that needs an answer first, how do we know that sheet of paper is what it claims to be?

Every object sold as art carries two histories at once. One is the history of what it is, who made it, when, with what materials. The other is the history of where it passed through from the moment it left the studio until today, who owned it, which shows it hung in, which inventory it turned up in. That second history is called provenance, and it is what backs up the first. A painting without a clear provenance is not necessarily fake, but every missing link in that chain is a place where someone could have slipped in a copy, or where nobody can yet prove the attribution is correct. For the biggest artists there is also a central document, the list a team of specialists compiles of every work they recognize as genuine by that artist, complete with its provenance record, exhibition history, and literature. That list is called a catalogue raisonné. Being on that list does not guarantee the price, but being left off it, or not being able to locate the work there, is a red flag any serious auction house checks before accepting a consignment. And sometimes that gap in the chain hides more than an authenticity question, because it can conceal a work that was stolen or confiscated at some point in its history, and that kind of legal claim can surface many years later and stop a sale that already looked closed.

In April of twenty twenty four a case happened that shows how this plays out in practice. At the SP-Arte fair in São Paulo, a gallery was privately offering a previously uncatalogued painting attributed to Tarsila do Amaral, the Brazilian artist who today holds the record for Brazilian painting, eleven million two hundred thousand dollars paid in December of twenty twenty for her work A Caipirinha. The painting at the fair, a landscape from nineteen twenty five, was priced at around three million two hundred thousand dollars. Right there, the founder of a rival auction house publicly accused it of being fake, without having seen it up close, comparing it to a counterfeit bill. The gallery selling it responded that it was impossible to judge authenticity without examining the work in person, and that it had not even been shown at the fair's official booth but in private meetings with buyers. The dispute stayed open for several months, until in August the only expert authorized by the entity that manages the artist's estate carried out a full authentication process, with scanning, X-rays, and pigment analysis, and concluded the work was genuine, with a new valuation of sixty million reais, the highest ever placed on one of her paintings. But that did not fully close the story, because a member of the artist's own family later came out publicly against that same authentication, even though it came from the one expert authorized to certify her work. Notice what happened there. Nobody settled the doubt by looking at the signature or asking the previous owner. On the technical side, it got settled with science applied to the physical object, comparing pigments, layers, and technique against what is known of the artist's actual method, though even that was not enough to bring everyone involved into agreement.

What we know is that there was a public accusation, a technical authentication that declared the work genuine, and part of the family that still does not accept it. What is reasonable to think is that this kind of dispute will keep showing up in markets where an artist's supply is small and demand grew fast, because that is where forging is more tempting and harder for a buyer to check against other known pieces. What this case cannot prove is that a technical authentication closes the discussion forever, or that an accusation like this always gets resolved so fast. A serious authentication process takes weeks or months, costs money, and needs physical access to the work, and even then it can still coexist with a family disagreement that stays open.

Back to your client and the artist's proof. What you can tell her with confidence is the definition, an artist's proof is a copy set apart from the normal numbered edition, historically reserved for the artist, and it may or may not be worth more depending on the specific case, not as a general rule. What you need to find out before weighing in on price is whether that sheet is actually recorded as an artist's proof in the edition's records, with an invoice or documentation from the dealer who originally sold it, and whether the paper, the size, and the signature match what is known about the other copies from that same plate. That is where being careful matters more than at any other point in the conversation. Do not tell your client something is authentic because the dealer says so, tell her to get the provenance documentation in writing before moving the price up, and if the sum is large, that it is worth consulting an independent specialist before closing any deal. One possible line with the end client is simple, you can tell him with confidence where an artist's proof sits in an edition's hierarchy, and you have to tell him just as clearly that confirming whether that specific sheet is what it claims to be is still pending, before talking about what it is worth.
