That same institutional logic is what we're going to see at work today in Brazil, in Venezuela, and in Colombia.

A client writes to you after visiting a fair over the weekend. She saw a Gego sculpture, metal threads forming a net in the air, and right next to it a Soto mobile, rods that shift with the air moving through the room. She asks if it's the same thing, because both struck her as sculptures that breathe. You know they're not the same family, and you have to explain why before she walks away thinking all Latin American geometric art is interchangeable.

Here it's worth separating two distinct ways of working with geometric abstraction, because at first glance they blur together. A group of Brazilian artists, among them Lygia Clark and Hélio Oiticica, started from São Paulo's concrete art movement and broke with it in the late nineteen fifties, pushing the work to stop being a fixed object made to hang and start activating through the viewer's body, a Clark Bicho you fold with your hands, an Oiticica Parangolé you wear like a cape. That's known as Neo-Concretism. In parallel, in Venezuela, Jesús Rafael Soto and Carlos Cruz-Diez worked a different idea, optical movement, sculptures and paintings that change depending on the angle you view them from, or as you move in front of them, what's called kinetic art. Both movements were born almost at the same time and share a vocabulary of lines, geometry, and pure color, but one activates the work through touch and the other through shifting vision, and that's the distinction you need to be able to explain when a client lumps them into the same sentence.

The case that best shows how this turns into a market is Olga de Amaral, Colombian, who works geometry through fiber and weaving instead of metal or paint. In November two thousand twenty five, Christie's sold her piece Pueblo H for three million one hundred twenty five thousand dollars, an artist record. That result didn't come out of nowhere. Between two thousand twenty five and two thousand twenty six, at least six of her pieces sold between six hundred ninety eight thousand five hundred dollars and that record, spread across several houses, and before all of that, between two thousand twenty four and two thousand twenty five, the Fondation Cartier in Paris, a private foundation, gave her her first major European retrospective. The museum validated her first, the price rose after, and the price held up through several sales, not just one.

As a contrast, there's Gego, the German Venezuelan artist behind the metal net your client saw. Her institutional résumé is every bit as strong as Amaral's, a major touring retrospective organized among the Guggenheim Bilbao, the Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum in New York, Museo Jumex in Mexico, and MASP in São Paulo. But her auction record still sits at one million five hundred twelve thousand five hundred dollars, from two thousand seventeen, and since then there haven't been enough comparable public sales to talk about a trend the way you can with Amaral. Same kind of institutional recognition, a very different market result so far.

That's the limit you need to draw. What you know is that a major retrospective at a serious museum gives an artist a validation no single auction result can match on its own. What's reasonable to think is that validation eventually pushes the price up over time, the way it did with Amaral. What you still can't claim about Gego is that her next piece at auction will repeat that same jump, because the market hasn't yet shown the repetition that Amaral's has, and a retrospective is a signal, not a guarantee.

Here's roughly how you answer your client. "They share an era and neighboring countries, but not technique, Gego's piece is built through how you look at it, and Soto's changes with your own movement in front of it, and putting them in the same sentence makes you sound like you don't know the material." You can say that with full confidence. You can also say that both movements, plus Amaral's fiber work, carry strong institutional backing today, from the Guggenheim to the Jumex to the Fondation Cartier itself. What you need to check before pricing anything is the specific track record of that individual piece, because a unique Gego sculpture doesn't compare to a Soto edition or a large Amaral tapestry, they're markets with very different depth even when they share a wall at the same fair. And where you want to be careful is promising that client that a Gego piece will move in the market the way Amaral's has, because so far there's no evidence that it is.
