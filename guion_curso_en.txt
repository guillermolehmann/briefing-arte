Week four, lesson one.

Yesterday's question. Marisol, the collector with the inherited Soto, told you something at the end you didn't expect... what mattered most to her, selling fast or where the piece ended up?... Where it ended up. She told you she wasn't in a hurry to sell, that what mattered more was the sculpture landing in a collection or a museum that would show it, and that the money came second.

That comment is today's way in. Picture this, a Friday afternoon, two different clients call asking about the same oil painting hanging in your viewing room. The first wants to know everything about the artist's training and the context of the work before deciding anything, and asks you to send the catalogue raisonné if one exists. The second doesn't even let you finish saying the artist's name, and asks straight out whether the painting fits the new wall in the living room and matches the rest of the decor. It's the same work, it's the same price, and you need two completely different conversations for the two of them, even though both, if it goes well, end in the same sale.

What separates those two clients is why they collect, and that reason changes everything you need to tell them. Some people spend weeks reading about an artist before buying a single piece of theirs, some measure the wall before asking the price, some ask first how much a work might be worth in five years, and some buy to be in the photo at a museum gala. Those four ways of approaching a work are known as the passionate collector, the decorator, the investor, and the social collector, and in practice almost nobody is purely one of the four. Most of your clients blend two or three in shifting proportions, and your job is to hear which one carries the most weight in any given conversation before you open your mouth, because the same argument that wins over a passionate collector, the artist's trajectory and where the work sits in their career, can bore stiff someone who only wants to know if the frame matches the rug.

The passionate collector, taken to the extreme, shows up in Eduardo Costantini, the Argentine businessman who founded MALBA, Buenos Aires's museum of Latin American art, with his own collection twenty five years ago. In December of twenty twenty five he announced he had bought the entire Daros Latinamerica collection, based in Zurich, one thousand two hundred and thirty three works by one hundred and seventeen artists, among them Lygia Clark, Hélio Oiticica, Gego, and the same Jesús Rafael Soto behind Marisol's sculpture. That single purchase doubles MALBA's holdings, bringing them to roughly three thousand works, and it landed right before the museum's twenty fifth anniversary, so the museum has already announced an expansion under the neighboring plaza just to show it all. Costantini didn't buy those one thousand two hundred and thirty three works planning to resell them, he bought them to complete the story his museum has been telling since it opened, and that's the mark of the passionate collector, the work joins a narrative the owner has been building long before that particular purchase.

Set that against Jorge Pérez, the Miami real estate developer who that same December gave eighty two works from his personal collection to the museum that carries his name, the Pérez Art Museum Miami. The gift was mostly photography, with a strong presence of Latin American art and work from the African diaspora. Pérez collects with real passion too, but the act of giving in public, with his own name on the museum's facade, adds a layer Costantini's case doesn't show as strongly, building a visible place inside the social and institutional ecosystem of his own city. Neither one is more genuine than the other, they're two different ways the same passion can end up building something larger than a private collection.

What you can't take from these two cases is that every Latin American collector ends up donating to a museum or buying entire collections. Costantini and Pérez are two exceptional figures, with the kind of wealth the vast majority of your clients don't have, and the typology is there to help you listen better, not to guess in advance what someone will do based on their last name or their country of origin. You also can't assume a client who opens the conversation talking about return on investment is purely an investor, that same person might end the conversation talking about the artist with an enthusiasm you didn't see coming.

With a new Latin American client there are three more things worth keeping in mind, alongside the typology. The first is family, because in this region collecting tends to be a joint project or something passed down across generations, so asking who else weighs in on the decision saves you surprises, like the case of Isabel and Agustín Coppel, the Mexican couple who build their collection together and connect it to Americas Society in New York. The second is the city, because Miami today holds the densest network of Latin American collectors and foundations, while New York holds the greater institutional weight, MoMA, the Met, the Guggenheim, and knowing which city your client lives in tells you which institutions they may already have ties to. The third is language, because a good number of these clients prefer, even when their English is flawless, to have the conversation about price and about art explained to them in Spanish, and that one choice builds a trust that a vice chairman at a big house doesn't always offer them in their own tongue. Family, city, and language together add a second layer to the typology, because a passionate collector from Buenos Aires and one from Miami arrive at that same passion through different networks of trust, and you need both readings together to understand the whole person in front of you.

Back to that Friday afternoon call. The client who asked if the painting matched the living room writes to you again the next day, and this time asks if that same work has room to rise in price over the next few years... Do you still think this one's purely a decorator, or does the conversation change?... Sit with that until tomorrow, we'll pick it up then.
