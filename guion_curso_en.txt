The client with the small gallery's certificate of authenticity calls you again. He got a second opinion, and this time the piece checked out, an independent specialist confirmed the signature and materials match the artist's period. Now he wants to consign it with Phillips, and he asks you something you never fully explained, what actually happens when a work enters a consignment, how much the house keeps, who decides the minimum price, and whether there is a way to guarantee some money even if the auction goes badly.

Signing a consignment means signing a contract, one that goes well beyond simply asking the house to sell something, and like any contract it splits money and risk between two parties. The house and the owner agree in advance on a minimum price below which the work will not sell, called the reserve. If the hammer does not reach that reserve, the piece goes unsold and returns to its owner. On the hammer that is reached, the house takes its cut, the seller's commission, which at Sotheby's has a published number, a standard ten percent of the hammer, with a five hundred dollar minimum on small lots and an extra two percent if the result beats the agreed high estimate. Christie's and Phillips do not publish a fixed number, they negotiate it case by case depending on the work's value and category. Now, when a house really wants an important piece, it can offer the owner something stronger than the reserve alone, a guarantee, a minimum price the house assures regardless of what happens at auction. If the hammer does not reach that floor, the house still pays the owner that money, and often ends up owning the work itself. That takes most of the risk off the seller and shifts it onto the house, or sometimes onto a third party who steps in to share that bet in exchange for a fee of its own, known as a third-party guarantee. A smaller version of the same idea is a commitment signed before the sale, someone commits in writing to bid up to a certain level, ensuring the work has demand from the moment the evening starts. That commitment is called an irrevocable bid, because whoever signs it is obligated to buy if nobody bids higher.

At Sotheby's Modern Evening Auction in May of twenty twenty six, Henri Matisse's La Séance du matin went up for sale marked with two symbols in the catalogue at once, guaranteed property and irrevocable bid. That means Sotheby's had assured the owner a price floor before the auction, and that someone had also committed in writing to bid up to a set level, guaranteeing the work would sell that night no matter what. Under the published standard commission, if that piece had hammered at, say, ten million dollars, the house would have kept a million dollars in commission from the seller, plus another two hundred thousand if the result beat the high estimate.

A different case, to mark the contrast, is an untitled work by Mark Grotjahn, an American abstract painter, that Christie's sold under the third-party guarantee symbol. There, the house did not put up its own money as the floor, an outside investor took on that bet in exchange for a fee, and the piece sold for one million three thousand three hundred dollars. The difference with the Matisse is not visible in the final result, it shows in who carries the risk if the auction goes badly, the house itself in one case, an outside third party in the other.

Neither the guarantee nor the irrevocable bid means the work is sold in advance at a fixed price. The auction still runs with real buyers who can bid above that floor, and in fact that is what usually happens with the best pieces. What does change is that the owner already knows they will collect something no matter what, and that the buyer of the piece has a right to know that financial interest from the house or a third party exists, which is why the symbol is printed in the catalogue. There is also no single commission number that is the same across all three houses, only Sotheby's publishes its own, the other two negotiate, so any figure you give a client about what Christie's or Phillips charges has to come from talking to that specific house, not from a fixed table.

With your certificate client, you can now explain the full process. You can tell him with confidence that the reserve protects an agreed minimum price, that the seller's commission will depend on the house and the value of the piece, and that if Phillips considers the work genuinely strong, it can offer him a guarantee that removes the risk before the night of the auction. What you need to make clear is that none of those terms get negotiated over the phone with you, the specialist department decides them after evaluating the piece up close, and that the second opinion he already got helps but does not replace the review Phillips itself will run before signing anything.
